Chapter 1 of 5 · Definition

Defining jurisprudence, and why nobody agrees

About 18 minutes · 1 interactive explorer · 5-question check

Here's something that confused me the first time I opened Paranjape: the chapter on "definition" gives you a dozen definitions, and they don't agree with each other. Some are so wide they include religion. Some are so narrow they only cover the law courts apply.

It's tempting to treat that list as a memorisation chore. Twelve names, twelve quotes, cram the night before. But there's a much better way to hold it in your head, and once you see it, the list starts to make sense. That's my goal for this chapter.

Who this is for

LLB and BA LLB students reading Unit I of Studies in Jurisprudence & Legal Theory. You don't need any jurisprudence background. If you've studied Contract or Constitutional Law, you have everything you need.

Start with the word itself

"Jurisprudence" comes from the Latin jurisprudentia. Split it in two and it explains itself:

jurisfrom jus: law, or a right. "Of law."
prudentiaknowledge, skill, foresight. The root of the English word "prudence".
= knowledge of lawor "skill in law". This is the starting point of almost every exam answer.

So far so good. But "knowledge of law" has a problem. A lawyer who knows every section of the CPC has knowledge of law. Is that jurisprudence? No. Jurisprudence is knowledge about law. It takes one step back.

Think about cricket. Knowing the LBW rule is knowing the law of cricket. Asking why we let an umpire decide, what makes a rule binding on players, or whether the LBW rule is fair to batters, that's jurisprudence of cricket. Same game, different altitude.

Sidenote

The word travels badly. In French, jurisprudence means case law, the body of court decisions. American writers sometimes use it the same way ("First Amendment jurisprudence"). In your Indian LLB paper it means the theory and philosophy of law. Keep the two apart.

Why is it so hard to define?

Paranjape is upfront about this: there is no single accepted definition. The reasons are worth knowing, because "why is it difficult to define jurisprudence?" turns up as a short question by itself.

  • "Law" has no agreed definition. Jurisprudence is defined in terms of law, so every disagreement about law becomes a disagreement about jurisprudence.
  • Jurists start from different places. One sees law as the command of a sovereign, another as the custom of a people, another as an instrument of justice. Each definition carries that starting point inside it.
  • Society keeps changing. What a Roman jurist thought belonged to law (religion, morals) is very different from what a 19th-century English positivist allowed in.
  • The word was used differently in different countries, as the sidenote above shows.

Put those together and you get the single most useful idea in this chapter:

Every definition of jurisprudence is secretly a definition of law.

When Austin says jurisprudence is about "positive law", he's telling you what he thinks law is. When Dias & Hughes say it's "any thought or writing about law", they're telling you law can't be cut off from everything around it. Read each definition as a window into the jurist's idea of law, and they stop being random.

The pattern: wide, then narrow, then wide again

The history of defining jurisprudence breathes in and out.

It starts wide. For Cicero and Ulpian, law was tangled up with religion, morality and justice. Ulpian's definition literally includes "things divine". Nobody saw a reason to separate them.

Then the positivists pull it tight. In the 18th and 19th centuries, Bentham and Austin wanted to make the study of law rigorous, almost scientific. To do that, they trimmed away everything that wasn't law "properly so called": morality, religion, custom the State hadn't adopted. Holland and Salmond tightened it further. Think of it as clearing the clutter off a desk so you can see what's actually on it.

Then it opens up again. By the 20th century, jurists like Pound, Stone and Dias argued that you can't understand law by staring only at its rules. Law lives in society; it's shaped by economics, psychology, politics. So the net widens again, this time to other disciplines rather than religion.

Exam lens

This three-beat story (wide → narrow → wide) makes a ready-made introduction or conclusion for any long answer on the definition of jurisprudence. It shows the examiner you understand why the definitions differ, not just what they say.

Three families of definitions

Here's another way to file them away. Instead of twelve separate quotes, remember three families. Each family shares an idea of what law is.

FamilyJuristsLaw is…Weak spot
Ancient / moralCicero, UlpianBound up with justice, morality and religionToo wide to mark out a legal discipline
Analytical / positivistBentham, Austin, Holland, Salmond, Gray, Keeton, AllenThe law that actually exists, laid down by the State or applied by courtsToo narrow; ignores morality, custom and social purpose
Modern / functionalPound, Paton, Stone, Dias & HughesA working institution inside society, to be studied with help from other disciplinesSo wide the boundaries blur

Allen isn't in the explorer above, but he's worth one line in an answer: he described jurisprudence as the scientific synthesis of the essential principles of law. File him with the analytical family.

Common mix-up

Austin and Holland both say "positive law", and students swap them constantly. Austin: the philosophy of positive law. Holland: the formal science of positive law. A silly line that helps: Austin philosophises, Holland formalises, Salmond goes civil.

So which definition is "right"?

None of them, completely. And that's an acceptable thing to say in an exam, as long as you then offer something better than a shrug.

After presenting the main definitions and their criticisms, end with a working definition that pulls the strands together. Here's one you can adapt (it's a synthesis, so present it as your own summary, not as a quote):

Jurisprudence is the systematic study of the general principles, concepts and theories of law: what law is, how it develops, what it ought to be, and how it works in society.

A working definition built from the jurists above

Notice how each phrase nods to a family. "What law is" is the analytical tradition. "How it develops" is the historical school. "What it ought to be" is the ethical side (Bentham's censorial jurisprudence). "How it works in society" is Pound and the sociologists. You'll meet all four again in the Scope chapter.

How to write it up

Etymology first. Then four to six definitions, grouped by family, each with a one-line criticism. Then the reason no single definition works. Then your working definition. Five definitions explained well beat twelve listed.
