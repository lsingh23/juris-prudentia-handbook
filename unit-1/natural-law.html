Natural law: the law above the law

About 24 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1976, during the Emergency, the Supreme Court was asked a stark question: if the right to move court for personal liberty is suspended, does a detenu have any remedy at all? The majority said no. Justice H.R. Khanna, dissenting, said that the right to life and liberty does not come from the Constitution in the first place; it existed before it.

That disagreement is a two-thousand-year-old argument in miniature. Is law simply whatever the State lays down? Or is there a higher law, discoverable by reason, against which every State's law can be measured, and found wanting? The second answer is natural law, and this chapter traces it from the Greeks to the Indian Supreme Court.

What "natural law" means

Natural law has gone by many names: the law of nature, divine law, the law of reason, universal law, jus naturale. Behind all of them sits one idea:

There are principles of right and justice that are higher than any State's law, and that human reason can discover.

From that idea follow its classic features. Natural law is said to be universal (the same everywhere), eternal or unchanging (at least in its classical form), discovered, not made (by reason, or by revelation), and a standard for judging positive law. That last feature is what makes it matter: if a State's law conflicts with natural law, the natural lawyer says the State's law is defective, and perhaps not law at all.

Positivist question

Was this rule made by the proper authority in the proper way? If yes, it is law, whatever its moral merits. Law as it is.

Natural law question

Is this rule consistent with reason and justice? If not, it lacks the full claim to be called law. Law as it ought to be.

You've met this split already, as Bentham's expository and censorial jurisprudence in the Nature chapter. Natural law is the censorial side turned into a whole theory of law.

Common mix-up

"Natural law" is not the law of physics, and it is not "natural justice". The law of gravity describes what does happen; natural law prescribes what ought to happen. Natural justice (hearing and no bias) is a set of procedural rules that grew out of natural law thinking; it's covered at the end of this chapter.

Two thousand years, explored

Paranjape divides the history into periods: ancient (Greek and Roman), medieval, the Renaissance and the age of reason, the decline in the nineteenth century, and the revival in the twentieth. The striking thing is not only what natural law said in each period but what it was used for. Sometimes it propped up kings and the Church; sometimes it fuelled revolutions. Step through the thinkers and watch its standing rise, crash, and recover.

The ancient period: Greece and Rome

Greece

Greek philosophers were the first to ask whether law is merely convention (made by people, different in every city) or rooted in nature (the same everywhere). The Sophists leaned towards convention: laws are made by the powerful for their own interest. Socrates answered that humans have an insight, a moral sense, by which they can know good and evil; and he accepted death rather than disobey the laws of Athens. Plato located justice in an ideal order in which each class does its proper work.

Aristotle gave the distinction its classic form. In the Nicomachean Ethics he separated natural justice, which has the same force everywhere and does not depend on what people think, from conventional (legal) justice, which is fixed by each community. Man is part of nature in two ways: a creature like any other, and a rational being who can tell right from wrong. Aristotle also used "nature" to justify slavery, an early example of natural law serving the existing order.

The Stoics (founded by Zeno) turned this into a universal ethic: the whole universe is governed by reason, and to live according to nature is to live according to reason. Since reason is common to all people, the Stoics spoke of a single law for all humankind, an idea that travelled straight to Rome.

Rome

The Romans made natural law practical. Cicero's statement in De Republica is the most quoted line in the whole topic:

True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting. It will not be one law at Rome and another at Athens, but one eternal and unchangeable law, valid for all nations and all times.

Cicero, De Republica

Roman jurists divided law into three:

jus civileThe civil law of Rome, applying only to Roman citizens.
jus gentiumLaw common to all peoples, applied by the praetor to foreigners and in dealings with them.
jus naturaleThe law of nature, ideal and universal. Gaius equated it with jus gentium; Ulpian said it was common to all animals.

The praetors used jus gentium and natural reason to soften the rigid old civil law. That is the lasting Roman lesson: natural law can be an engine of legal development, not just a philosophers' ideal.

The medieval period: the Church and Aquinas

In the Middle Ages, natural law was absorbed into Christian theology. The Church Fathers, notably St Augustine, taught that natural law is God's law written in human hearts, and that a law which is not just does not seem to be a law at all (lex iniusta non est lex). Natural law now served the authority of the Church, which claimed to interpret God's will above the rulers of States.

St Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), in the Summa Theologica, produced the most complete system. He described four kinds of law, from the highest down:

  1. 01Lex aeterna · eternal law

    God's reason governing the whole universe. Known fully only to God.

  2. 02Lex naturalis · natural law

    The share of eternal law that rational creatures grasp through reason. Its first principle: do good and avoid evil.

  3. 03Lex divina · divine law

    Law revealed through scripture, to guide humans where reason alone falls short.

  4. 04Lex humana · human law

    Positive law made by rulers for the common good. Valid only so far as it agrees with natural law; an unjust law is "not a law but a perversion of law".

Aquinas was careful, though. He did not license disobedience whenever a law seemed unfair. An unjust law may still be obeyed to avoid scandal or disorder, unless it commands something against divine law. This balance is why his theory lasted: reason gives the content, but order is kept.

Exam lens

The four laws of Aquinas are a guaranteed short question and a mandatory paragraph in any long answer on natural law. Draw them as a stepped list or a pyramid: eternal at the top, human at the bottom, with the rule that human law must conform to natural law.

The Renaissance and the social contract

From the 15th century, the Reformation, the rise of nation-states and new science broke the Church's hold. Natural law became secular and rational: grounded in human reason, not theology. And it became the language of individual rights.

Grotius

Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), the Dutch jurist, is called the father of international law. In De Jure Belli ac Pacis (On the Law of War and Peace, 1625) he based the law between nations on natural law, and made the famous claim that natural law would still be valid even if we conceded that God does not exist (etiamsi daremus). Natural law rests on human nature, especially our social instinct (appetitus societatis). From it he derived principles such as pacta sunt servanda: agreements must be kept. Grotius also held that once people transfer power to a ruler by contract, they cannot take it back, so his theory supported established rulers.

The social contract thinkers

Three thinkers asked the same question: what were people like before there was a State, and why did they agree to create one? They gave three very different answers, and each answer supported a different kind of government. Compare them.

After them, Kant grounded law in the autonomy of the rational will, and Pufendorf and others refined natural rights. By the end of the 18th century, natural law had written itself into the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789). It was at the height of its power, and about to fall.

The decline in the nineteenth century

Within a few decades, natural law went from the language of revolutions to something most serious jurists dismissed. The reasons are a standard short question:

  • Analytical positivism. Bentham called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts". Austin separated law as it is from law as it ought to be, and confined jurisprudence to positive law, the command of the sovereign.
  • The historical school. Savigny argued that law grows from the Volksgeist of each people. Law is national and changing, not universal and eternal.
  • Hume's is/ought problem. You cannot logically derive what ought to be from what is. Natural law seemed to do exactly that, reading moral rules off "nature".
  • The spirit of science. The 19th century trusted observation and experiment. Principles "discovered by reason" looked like personal opinion dressed up.
  • Its own vagueness. Natural law had been used to justify absolute monarchy (Hobbes) and revolution (Rousseau), slavery (Aristotle) and equality. A theory that supports everything seems to prove nothing.
  • The strong nation-state and codification. With codes like the Napoleonic Code, law seemed complete; there was no need to look above it.

Alf Ross, a later critic, put the last point brutally: like a harlot, natural law is at the disposal of everyone.

The revival in the twentieth century

Natural law came back because positivism, taken alone, seemed to have no answer to the horrors of the century. Nazi Germany operated through formally valid laws. At Nuremberg, defendants pleaded that they had obeyed the law of their State. The reply had to be that some "laws" are so unjust that obeying them is no defence. The German jurist Radbruch, once a positivist, concluded that statutes which are intolerably unjust lose their character as law.

Other reasons for the revival: the failure of purely formal law to deliver justice in an industrial society; the sociological school's interest in the purposes of law; and the post-war human rights movement, culminating in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). But the revived natural law is different: more modest, often relative to time and place, and often procedural.

Stammler: natural law with a variable content

Rudolf Stammler (1856–1938), a neo-Kantian, gave the revival its slogan: natural law with a variable content. There is no single eternal code; the content of just law must change with the conditions of each society. What is constant is the method, the idea of justice by which each society's law is judged. His ideal was a community of free-willing persons, in which each respects the other as an end. Kohler, a neo-Hegelian, similarly made law relative to the civilisation of each age.

Fuller: the inner morality of law

Lon L. Fuller, the American jurist, argued in The Morality of Law (1964) that law has an internal morality. Any system that tries to guide conduct by rules must meet certain conditions, or it fails to be a legal system at all. He told the parable of King Rex, who failed at law-making in eight different ways. Each failure is the mirror image of one of Fuller's eight principles. Fuller called this procedural natural law: it says nothing directly about the content of laws, only about the conditions a legal system must satisfy. His debate with H.L.A. Hart in the Harvard Law Review (1958), over whether Nazi law was really law, is the classic modern exchange between natural law and positivism. Hart, though a positivist, conceded a "minimum content of natural law": given human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources and limited understanding and strength of will, every workable legal system must protect persons, property and promises.

Finnis: basic goods

John Finnis, in Natural Law and Natural Rights (1980), restated the Thomist tradition in modern, secular terms. There are seven basic goods, self-evidently valuable for any human being:

  • Life
  • Knowledge
  • Play
  • Aesthetic experience
  • Sociability (friendship)
  • Practical reasonableness
  • Religion

The goods are not ranked; each is fundamental. To pursue them sensibly we need practical reasonableness, which Finnis breaks into nine requirements (for example, a coherent plan of life, no arbitrary preference among goods or persons, respect for every basic good in every act, following one's conscience). Law exists to secure the common good, the conditions in which people can pursue these goods. Unjust laws are law in a secondary, watered-down sense: Finnis rejects the crude reading of lex iniusta non est lex.

Natural law in India

Indian thought had its own higher-law tradition long before the Greeks were read here. In ancient Indian jurisprudence, dharma bound even the king; the ruler was not above the law but its servant, and the cosmic order of rita stood behind it. Paranjape notes that this plays much the same role as natural law in the West.

In modern Indian law, natural law works through three channels: the Constitution's fundamental rights, the Supreme Court's reading of Article 21, and the principles of natural justice.

From Gopalan to Maneka: Article 21

Positivist reading

A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras

1950

The majority read "procedure established by law" in Article 21 as any procedure laid down by a validly enacted statute. The framers had deliberately avoided American "due process". Law meant the law enacted by the State: a strictly positivist view.

Natural law reading

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India

1978

The Court held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 must be read together. It also held that the principle of audi alteram partem applied to impounding a passport. In effect, natural justice and due process were read into the Constitution.

Limits on the sovereign

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

1973

Parliament's power to amend the Constitution does not extend to destroying its basic structure. Earlier, in Golaknath (1967), Subba Rao C.J. had described fundamental rights as the modern name for natural rights. Kesavananda put a higher-law limit on even the constituent power.

Rights before the Constitution

K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India

2017

A nine-judge bench held privacy a fundamental right and described life and personal liberty as inalienable rights inherent in human existence, recognised rather than created by the Constitution. It declared the majority in ADM Jabalpur (1976) wrong and vindicated Khanna J.'s dissent.

After Maneka Gandhi, the Court drew a long line of rights out of Article 21: speedy trial, free legal aid, livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985), a clean environment, dignity. Each step is a judge reading what law ought to protect into what the text is, which is natural law reasoning in a positivist constitution.

The principles of natural justice

The most practical legacy of natural law in Indian courts is the doctrine of natural justice, applied to courts, tribunals and administrative authorities alike. It has two classic rules, and Indian law adds a third:

Nemo judex in causa suaNo one should be a judge in their own cause: the rule against bias (personal, pecuniary, or as to subject matter)A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969): a selection board member was himself a candidate; the selection was quashed

A.K. Kraipak matters for a second reason: the Court held that the line between quasi-judicial and administrative functions is thin, and that natural justice applies to administrative action too. The rules are flexible, not rigid: in Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985), the Court accepted that they can be excluded by the Constitution or a statute in exceptional situations, such as under the second proviso to Article 311(2).

How to write it up

Meaning and features first. Then the history in periods: Greek, Roman, medieval (Aquinas's four laws), Renaissance (Grotius; Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau in a table), the 19th-century decline with reasons, the 20th-century revival (Stammler, Fuller, Finnis, Hart's minimum content). Then India: dharma, Gopalan to Maneka, Kesavananda, natural justice. End with an assessment: natural law keeps returning because positive law alone cannot tell us whether a law is just.

An honest assessment

The criticisms are real: natural law is vague (whose reason?), subjective (it often reflects the values of its author), logically suspect (Hume's is/ought gap), and politically elastic (it has served kings and revolutionaries alike). Positivists add that it confuses two questions that should be kept apart: what the law is, and whether it is good.

Yet it refuses to die, and the reason is in this chapter's opening case. When a legal system produces ADM Jabalpur, something outside the system is needed to say that it went wrong. Natural law supplies the vocabulary of rights, dignity and fairness that positive law then borrows. That is why the Indian Constitution, a thoroughly positive document, is today read through ideas that Cicero and Aquinas would recognise.
