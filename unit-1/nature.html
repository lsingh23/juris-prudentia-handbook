Chapter 2 of 5 · Nature

What kind of subject is jurisprudence?

About 16 minutes · 2 interactive widgets · 4-question check

Open any other paper on your timetable and you know what you're getting. Contract has the Indian Contract Act. Criminal law has the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. There are sections, illustrations, leading cases. Then you open jurisprudence, and there's… none of that.

No bare act. No "Section 7 of Jurisprudence". It can feel slippery, like there's nothing solid to hold on to. This chapter is about exactly that feeling: what kind of subject is this, and how is it different from the rest of your law degree? That's what examiners mean by its "nature".

Law, versus thinking about law

Here's the mental model I'd like you to carry through this chapter. Every other subject you study is law. Jurisprudence is about law.

Contract, Torts, BNS, CPC

A map of one city. It tells you where the roads go: which agreement is void, which act is an offence, which court has jurisdiction.

Jurisprudence

The study of maps. What makes something a map at all? Who has the authority to draw one? Is this road in the right place? Should it exist?

Once you see it this way, most of the "features" listed in textbooks fall out naturally. Let's go through them.

Six features of its nature

1. It isn't codified

Paranjape stresses this point: jurisprudence is not a codified subject. There is no statute to quote, so your answers are judged on the quality of your reasoning and your command of the jurists, not on recalling a section number. That's freeing, once you get used to it.

2. It studies law in general, not one country's law

Remember Paton from Chapter 1? Jurisprudence studies "the general notion of law itself". Concepts like right, duty, ownership, possession and legal personality exist in every legal system. Jurisprudence studies them as ideas, not as they happen to appear in one statute.

3. It asks both "what is" and "what ought to be"

This one comes from Bentham, and it's worth getting really comfortable with, because it shows up in the nature, the scope and the schools of jurisprudence.

  • Expository jurisprudence describes law as it is. It's a statement of fact you could check.
  • Censorial jurisprudence evaluates law as it ought to be. Bentham also called it the science of legislation, because it's about what lawmakers should do.

Sounds simple. But in practice, students blur the two all the time. Try sorting these statements.

Try itIs, or ought?Is each statement describing the law, or judging it?

    The trick is to look for words like should, ought, must be changed, unfair. Those signal a judgment. A statement about what the law currently says, even a surprising one, is still expository.

    4. It's abstract, but not useless

    Jurisprudence deals in abstractions. But those abstractions decide real cases. Whether a judge reads "procedure established by law" as any procedure Parliament enacts, or as a fair procedure, is a jurisprudential choice. We'll see exactly that in the Scope chapter, with real Supreme Court judgments.

    5. It's interdisciplinary

    To understand why a law exists and whether it works, jurisprudence borrows from sociology, economics, history, ethics, psychology and political science. Julius Stone's phrase for this was "the lawyer's extraversion": the lawyer turning to look outward.

    6. It's dynamic

    Because it's about law in society, jurisprudence keeps growing as society changes. Questions about privacy, environmental rights, or who is responsible when software makes a decision weren't on the syllabus a century ago. The subject has no fixed edge, which is a theme we'll pick up in Scope.

    The big question: is jurisprudence a science?

    Austin, Holland, Salmond, Gray and Pound all use the word "science" in their definitions. So is it one? This is a favourite exam question, and the answer is a careful "yes, in one sense".

    First, what do we mean by science? In the broad sense, a science is a systematic, organised body of knowledge, built by observation and reasoning, that yields general principles. Physics fits. Does jurisprudence?

    Both sides are right about different things. Jurisprudence is not an exact or natural science. But it is a science in the older, broader sense: a systematic body of knowledge (Latin scientia simply means knowledge). Because it studies human conduct and institutions in society, the most accurate label is a social science.

    Notice the scale ends roughly level if you place everything. That's the honest answer, and it's what makes for a strong exam conclusion: jurisprudence is a science in the sense of systematic knowledge, and more precisely a social science, but not an exact science like physics.

    Sidenote

    When Austin and Salmond called jurisprudence a "science", they were using the older meaning of the word: any organised body of knowledge, from scientia. They weren't claiming it works like chemistry. Pointing this out in an answer shows you've understood the debate rather than picked a side blindly.

    "Jurisprudence is the eye of law"

    You'll meet this phrase in Indian textbooks and question papers, often as a "discuss" question. It sounds poetic, so let's make it concrete by following a single idea around a loop. Step through it.

    Step throughHow the eye of law works

    So, in two directions, an eye lets a body see:

    Inward

    Jurisprudence lets law see its own foundations: what a right is, what makes a rule valid, where law comes from. The basic concepts every other subject quietly relies on.

    Outward

    It lets law see its surroundings: society, morality, economics, the people it governs. Whether it's achieving its purpose. This is Stone's "extraversion".

    Without the eye, law still functions, but blindly. Courts would apply rules without understanding why, and legislators would make rules without asking whether they're just. That's your answer in miniature.

    Jurisprudence or legal theory?

    Paranjape's title uses both terms, so it's fair to ask whether they differ. The term "legal theory" was popularised by W. Friedmann. Most Indian writers, including V.D. Mahajan, treat the two as near-synonyms, with jurisprudence as the slightly wider term: legal theory concentrates on the theories of what law is, while jurisprudence also covers legal concepts and the working of law. Two sentences on this is plenty in an exam.

    Recap: the nature in one glance

    • UncodifiedNo bare act; answers are reasoned, not recited.
    • GeneralStudies law as an idea, not one country's statutes (Paton).
    • Is + oughtExpository and censorial, both (Bentham).
    • AbstractTheoretical, yet decides real cases.
    • InterdisciplinaryBorrows from sociology, ethics, economics (Stone).
    • DynamicGrows as society changes.
    • Science?Systematic knowledge, a social science, not an exact one.
