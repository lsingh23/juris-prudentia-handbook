Chapter 3 of 5 · Scope

How far does jurisprudence reach?

About 20 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

If "nature" asks what kind of subject jurisprudence is, "scope" asks how much ground it covers. Where are its borders? What's inside, and what's left out?

Here's the short version of the story. Austin built a tight fence. Salmond drew a family tree. Modern jurists pulled the fence down. Once you have those three images, the rest of the chapter is detail you hang on them.

CompareThree borders around one subject

Austin's fence: positive law only

For Austin, the subject matter of jurisprudence was positive law: law set by political superiors to political inferiors, the commands of a sovereign backed by sanction. Everything else went outside the fence:

  • Morality, which he called positive morality
  • Divine law and religion
  • Custom, until the sovereign adopted it
  • International law, which he also classed as positive morality, since there's no sovereign above nations

Inside the fence, Austin made one more cut. He divided jurisprudence into two kinds.

General jurisprudenceParticular jurisprudence
StudiesPrinciples, notions and distinctions common to all (mature) legal systemsAny one actual system of law, or a part of it
ExampleThe concept of a right, or the distinction between written and unwritten lawThe law of contract in India; the law of England
QuestionWhat is ownership, anywhere?Who owns this land under Indian law?

The criticism

Austin's split was attacked from several sides, and the criticism is usually worth as many marks as the description:

  • Holland: only general jurisprudence can be a science. A science deals in general principles, so "particular jurisprudence" is a contradiction; it's really just the material a science works on.
  • "Mature" systems: Austin drew his general principles mainly from Roman and English law, which he considered mature. That leaves out most of the world's legal traditions, including ancient Indian law.
  • The line is blurry: in practice you study general concepts through a particular system. An Indian student learns "possession" through Indian cases.
  • The fence itself is too tight: by cutting out morality, custom and international law, Austin left out much of what shapes real legal systems.

Salmond's tree: generic and specific

Salmond organised the territory differently. He said "jurisprudence" can be used in two senses, and then split the narrower sense into three branches. This is a diagram worth drawing in your exam; examiners like it.

JurisprudenceSalmond
Generic senseThe science of every kind of law: civil, international, natural, moral
Specific senseThe science of civil law (the law of the land)
AnalyticalExpository or systematic. First principles of law as it is.
HistoricalHow law originated and developed over time.
EthicalScience of legislation. Law as it ought to be.

See how the three branches line up with things you already know? Analytical is Bentham's expository side. Ethical is his censorial side. Historical adds a third question: how did we get here?

Common mix-up

"Civil law" in Salmond doesn't mean civil as opposed to criminal. It means the law of the land, administered by the State through its courts. Criminal law is part of Salmond's civil law. Write this clarification in one line; it signals you've actually read Salmond.

The modern view: no fence at all

By the 20th century, the consensus had shifted. Paranjape's position, shared by Stone, Dias and others, is that the scope of jurisprudence cannot be confined within fixed limits. It grows as society grows, and it takes in anything that helps us understand law. A rough map of what's inside today:

Nature & sources of law

What law is; legislation, precedent and custom as sources.

Legal concepts

Rights and duties, persons, possession, ownership, property, obligations, liability.

Law & morality

Where they overlap, where they part, and which wins.

Law & State

Sovereignty, the source of legal authority, limits on power.

Law & society

How law shapes behaviour and is shaped by it.

Administration of justice

Civil and criminal justice, theories of punishment.

Schools of thought

Analytical, historical, philosophical, sociological, realist.

Justice & legal values

What law is for, and whether it achieves it.

That last-but-one box, the schools, deserves a proper look, because each school is really a different answer to "what should jurisprudence study?"

Sidenote

Paranjape also gives separate chapters to Natural Law theory and Indian legal theory, where law is closely tied to dharma, a duty-centred idea of right conduct. When you write about scope, a sentence noting that jurisprudence also covers non-Western traditions strengthens your criticism of Austin's "mature systems".

Why it matters: the utility of jurisprudence

Examiners often bundle "importance" or "utility" with scope. The textbook points are straightforward:

  • It clarifies basic concepts. Rights, possession, ownership and personality turn up in every other subject. Jurisprudence explains them once, properly.
  • It helps legislators. The ethical, censorial side asks what law ought to be, which is the starting point of every reform.
  • It helps judges in hard cases. When a statute is silent, vague or harsh, the judge's view of what law is shapes the outcome.
  • It helps lawyers argue from principle, not just from the nearest precedent.
  • It trains the mind. Salmond pointed to its intrinsic interest as a study in itself, and it sharpens logical, critical thinking.
  • It connects law to life, linking law to the social sciences.

The third point is the one most students state but can't illustrate. So here are three Supreme Court cases where jurisprudence quite literally decided the result.

Strict positivism

ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla

1976

During the Emergency, with enforcement of Article 21 suspended, the majority held that detenus could not go to court to challenge their detention. A strictly positivist reading: whatever the text currently allows is the law. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented. In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), the Supreme Court held the majority view was wrong and overruled it.

"Ought" read into "is"

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India

1978

The Court held that "procedure established by law" in Article 21 cannot mean any procedure; it must be fair, just and reasonable. A value judgment about what law ought to be became part of what the law is.

Limits on law-making

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

1973

Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure. The Court reasoned about what a constitution fundamentally is, which is a jurisprudential question if ever there was one.

Exam lens

One or two of these cases in a "scope and utility" answer lifts it well above the standard bullet list. Keep each to two sentences: what the Court held, and which jurisprudential idea drove it.
