Chapter 4 of 5 · Sources

Where does law come from?

About 22 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

A judge in Lucknow decides a property dispute. She applies a rule. Where did that rule come from? Maybe Parliament wrote it into a statute. Maybe the Supreme Court laid it down in a judgment twenty years ago. Maybe a community has followed it for so long that the courts now treat it as binding.

Those three answers, legislation, precedent and custom, are the three main sources of law, and together they make one of the most reliable long questions in the paper. This chapter gives you Salmond's classification first, then each source in turn, with the Indian position at every step.

Where this fits

Definition, nature and scope told you what jurisprudence studies. Sources is the first piece of the subject itself: it asks how a rule earns the right to be called law. You'll need it again in Unit II, because each school of legal theory has a favourite source. Austin backs legislation, Savigny backs custom, the realists back the courts.

What does "source of law" even mean?

The phrase sounds obvious, but it hides at least three different questions, and jurists talk past each other when they mix them up:

  • Where does law get its authority? From the State, from the sovereign, from God, from the people's acceptance.
  • Where does law get its content? From a statute book, a law report, a commentary, a religious text, a community's practice.
  • What caused the law to be what it is? Social needs, economic pressure, a reform movement, a foreign model.

Salmond's achievement was to separate these questions and give each a name. That's why his classification is the starting point of almost every answer.

A source of law is wherever a rule gets either its force or its content.

Salmond's classification

Salmond first divides sources into formal and material, then splits material sources into legal and historical. Draw this tree in your exam; it takes a minute and examiners look for it.

Sources of lawSalmond
FormalWhere a rule gets its force and validity: the will of the State, expressed through its courts
MaterialWhere a rule gets its content or matter, not its validity
LegalAuthoritative. Recognised by the law itself; courts must follow them.
LegislationPrecedentCustomAgreement
HistoricalUnauthoritative. Influence the law in fact, but have no binding claim.
Juristic writingsForeign judgmentsReform reports

Salmond's own image is that legal sources are the gates through which new principles enter the law. Historical sources stand outside the gate. A jurist's book can persuade a judge; once the judge adopts its idea in a judgment, the idea has entered through the gate of precedent and become law.

The criticism, and Keeton's alternative

  • Allen objected that "the will of the State" explains very little. Custom, for instance, had binding force long before there was any State to will it.
  • The line between formal and material is hard to hold: a statute is both the source of a rule's validity and of its content.
  • Keeton offered a simpler working division: binding sources (legislation, judicial precedent, custom), which a judge must apply, and persuasive sources (professional opinion, principles of morality and equity, foreign judgments), which a judge may use when the binding sources run out.

Common mix-up

"Historical source" does not mean "old". A Law Commission report from last year is a historical source in Salmond's sense: it has no legal authority, however much it shapes the next statute. And a custom can be centuries old yet be a legal source, because the law itself tells courts to apply it.

    Legislation: law made on purpose

    Salmond defines legislation as that source of law which consists in the declaration of legal rules by a competent authority. It is the only source that is deliberate: someone sits down and decides what the law shall be. For analytical positivists like Austin, it is the source; everything else counts only if the sovereign permits it.

    The word is used in three senses: in the widest sense, every method by which law is made (including judicial decisions); in a narrower sense, any declaration of law by a competent body; and in the strict sense, law made by the legislature itself. Exam answers use the strict sense unless they say otherwise.

    Supreme and subordinate legislation

    Supreme legislation comes from the sovereign power in the State and cannot be repealed or controlled by any other legislative authority. In England that is Parliament. In India, Parliament and the State legislatures legislate within the limits set by the Constitution, so their Acts are open to judicial review: supreme in their field, but not above the Constitution.

    Subordinate legislation comes from an authority other than the sovereign and depends on it for its existence. Salmond lists five kinds:

    KindMade byExample
    ColonialLegislatures of dependent territoriesLaws of British-era legislatures, subject to Westminster
    Executive (delegated)Government departments under powers given by a statuteRules framed under the Motor Vehicles Act
    JudicialSuperior courts regulating their own procedureSupreme Court Rules under Article 145
    MunicipalLocal bodies for their areaMunicipal corporation bye-laws
    AutonomousBodies such as universities and corporationsA university's statutes and ordinances

    Delegated legislation is the kind that matters most in practice: modern government could not run without it. The Supreme Court settled its limits in In re Delhi Laws Act (1951): the legislature may delegate, but it cannot delegate its essential legislative function. It must lay down the policy and standards itself and leave only the details to the executive. Delegated legislation is controlled by the legislature (laying rules before the House), by procedure (publication, consultation) and by the courts, which strike down rules that go beyond the parent Act as ultra vires.

    Why legislation usually beats precedent

    Salmond compares the two and gives legislation the edge. These points are a standard short note:

    PointLegislationPrecedent
    Power to abolishCan repeal and abrogate as well as create (abrogative power)Can build on the law, but finds it hard to undo a settled rule
    Division of labourLegislature makes law, courts apply it (efficiency)The same judge both makes and applies the rule
    TimingDeclared in advance; people know the rule before they actMade after the event, then applied to the parties retrospectively
    CoverageCan provide for cases that have not yet arisenWaits for a dispute to reach court
    FormBrief, clear, published in one placeBuried in long judgments across many volumes of reports
    Where precedent winsCan be rigid and drafted far from real factsGrows out of real disputes; flexible and practical

    Precedent: law made by deciding cases

    A precedent is a judicial decision that is followed in later, similar cases. Salmond puts it as decisions of courts which contain in themselves a principle, and which are therefore cited as authority. The idea is simple fairness: like cases should be decided alike. It also gives the law certainty and saves judges from rethinking every question from scratch.

    Kinds of precedent

    • Declaratory and original. A declaratory precedent merely applies an existing rule; an original precedent creates and applies a new one. Only original precedents add to the law, so Salmond calls them the true source.
    • Authoritative and persuasive. An authoritative precedent must be followed by the judge, whether or not she agrees with it. A persuasive precedent need not be followed, but may be, on its merits: foreign judgments, decisions of other High Courts, Privy Council decisions today.
    • Absolutely and conditionally authoritative. Salmond's further split: an absolutely authoritative decision must be followed even if the later court thinks it wrong; a conditionally authoritative one may be disregarded in special circumstances, for example where it is plainly wrong or given without considering a binding authority.

    Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta

    Not everything a judge says becomes binding. Only the ratio decidendi, the principle of law on which the decision is based, binds later courts. Everything else is obiter dicta, "things said by the way": hypotheticals, observations, wider remarks the case did not need.

    Ratio decidendiObiter dicta
    MeaningThe reason for the decision; the rule needed to reach itRemarks made in passing, not needed for the decision
    ForceBinding on lower courts (authoritative)Persuasive only, however eminent the judge
    Found byReading the material facts with the decisionWhatever is left over

    Finding the ratio is a skill, and two tests are worth naming:

    • Wambaugh's reversal test. Take the proposition you think is the ratio and reverse it. If the decision would then have gone the other way, the proposition is the ratio. If the decision would stand anyway, it was obiter.
    • Goodhart's material facts test. The ratio is found in the facts the judge treated as material, together with the decision on those facts. A later case with the same material facts must reach the same result.

    Stare decisis, in England and in India

    The full maxim is stare decisis et non quieta movere: stand by what has been decided and do not disturb what is settled. It is precedent hardened into a duty. The English history in three dates:

    • 1898, London Street Tramways v. London County Council: the House of Lords held itself bound by its own decisions.
    • 1944, Young v. Bristol Aeroplane Co.: the Court of Appeal is bound by its own decisions, with three exceptions (conflicting decisions of its own, a decision inconsistent with a House of Lords ruling, and a decision given per incuriam).
    • 1966, the Practice Statement: the House of Lords announced it would depart from its own decisions "when it appears right to do so".

    In India the doctrine has a constitutional base. Article 141: the law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India. Article 144 adds that all authorities, civil and judicial, shall act in aid of the Supreme Court. The working rules that follow:

    Supreme Court and itself

    Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar

    1955

    "All courts" in Article 141 does not include the Supreme Court itself. The Court is not bound by its own earlier decisions and may depart from them when they are clearly wrong, though it does so with great caution.

    Bench strength

    Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v. State of Maharashtra

    2005

    A Constitution Bench summarised the rule: a decision of a larger bench binds any smaller bench. A bench of equal strength that doubts an earlier decision should not overrule it but refer the question to a larger bench.

    Prospective overruling

    I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab

    1967

    The Court borrowed the American doctrine of prospective overruling: a new rule applies to the future without unsettling transactions already completed under the old one. A good example of judges shaping how precedent itself works.

    Below the Supreme Court, a High Court's decisions bind every subordinate court within its territory, but are only persuasive for other High Courts. Within a High Court, a single judge is bound by a division bench. And in practice, Indian courts treat even considered obiter of the Supreme Court as binding on the courts below it. That is stricter than the English practice, and worth one line in an answer.

    Custom: law that grows

    Custom is the oldest source of all. Long before kings or parliaments, communities lived by practices they regarded as binding. Salmond's definition is the one to quote: custom is the embodiment of those principles which have commended themselves to the national conscience as principles of justice and public utility.

    Jurists disagree sharply about its status, which makes for a good paragraph:

    • Austin: custom is not law until the sovereign or the courts adopt it. Before that, it is only positive morality.
    • The historical school (Savigny): custom is the truest law, because it grows from the Volksgeist, the common consciousness of the people. Legislation should follow custom, not fight it.
    • The middle view (Salmond, Allen): custom is a genuine source of law, but in a modern State it becomes enforceable through recognition by the courts.

    Kinds of custom

    • Legal custom is binding by its own force.
      • Local custom prevails in a particular locality. In India the idea is wider: courts recognise customs of a caste, community, tribe or even a single family.
      • General custom prevails throughout the country. The English common law began as the general custom of the realm.
    • Conventional custom (usage) binds only because the parties are taken to have agreed to it, for example a trade usage read into a contract.

    The requisites of a valid custom

    This is the part examiners ask about most. A court will enforce an alleged custom only if it passes every one of these tests:

    • AntiquityIt must be ancient. English law requires "time immemorial", fixed at 1189 (the reign of Richard I). Indian law fixes no date; it is enough that the custom has been observed for a long time.
    • ContinuityIt must have been followed without interruption. A practice abandoned for a generation and then revived fails.
    • Peaceful enjoymentIt must have been exercised openly and as of right: not by force, not secretly, not by mere permission (nec vi, nec clam, nec precario).
    • CertaintyIts content and the people it binds must be definite. A vague practice cannot be enforced.
    • ReasonablenessIt must not be unreasonable. The test is not whether the court would have chosen it, but whether it offends justice and good sense.
    • ConsistencyIt must not conflict with other established customs of the same area or group.
    • Conformity with statuteNo custom can override an Act of the legislature, unless the Act itself saves it.
    • Morality and public policyIt must not be immoral or opposed to public policy.
    • Obligatory forcePeople must follow it because they feel bound to, not merely out of habit or convenience (opinio necessitatis).

    Custom in Indian law

    Custom has had unusual force in India, especially in personal law.

    Custom over text

    Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy

    Privy Council · 1868

    The Privy Council held that under the Hindu system of law, clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law. Custom could prevail even over the Smritis.

    Statutory definition

    Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, section 3(a)

    Statute

    Defines custom and usage as a rule which, having been continuously and uniformly observed for a long time, has obtained the force of law in a local area, tribe, community, group or family, provided it is certain, not unreasonable and not opposed to public policy. The Act's requisites mirror the textbook list almost exactly. Section 29(2) saves customary forms of divorce.

    Proof

    Ujagar Singh v. Mst. Jeo

    Supreme Court · 1959

    A custom must ordinarily be proved by the party relying on it. But once it has been repeatedly recognised by the courts, it may be judicially noticed without fresh proof.

    One more distinction, often asked as a short note: custom and prescription. Both rest on long practice. But custom creates a general rule of law for a class of people or a locality, while prescription creates a right for a particular person, such as an easement acquired by twenty years' peaceful use under the Indian Easements Act, 1882.

    The other sources

    Legislation, precedent and custom carry the long answer. These four round it off; give each a short paragraph.

    Juristic writings (professional opinion)

    In Rome, Augustus gave certain jurists the right to give opinions with the emperor's authority (jus respondendi), and the Law of Citations (426 AD) made the writings of five jurists, Papinian, Paul, Ulpian, Gaius and Modestinus, binding on judges. In England, Glanvill, Bracton, Coke and Blackstone shaped the common law. In India the commentaries went furthest: the Mitakshara of Vijnaneshwara and the Dayabhaga of Jimutavahana became the two great schools of Hindu law, so that a commentary effectively became the law itself. Today, writers such as Seervai or Basu are cited by Indian courts as persuasive authority: a historical source, in Salmond's terms.

    Equity

    Sir Henry Maine, in Ancient Law (1861), named three agencies by which law is brought into harmony with a changing society: legal fictions, equity and legislation, in that historical order. In England, equity grew in the Court of Chancery to soften the rigidity of the common law. In India, courts long applied "justice, equity and good conscience" where no rule existed, and many equitable principles now sit in statutes such as the Specific Relief Act and the Indian Trusts Act.

    Agreement (conventional law)

    Salmond counts agreement as a legal source: parties to a contract create rights and duties binding between themselves, and treaties between States are a major source of international law. The rule it produces binds only the parties, which is why it is sometimes left off shorter lists.

    Religion and morality

    Personal laws in India draw heavily on religious texts and the traditions built around them, and moral ideas enter through doctrines such as public policy and reasonableness. This link between law and morals returns in the next chapter, on natural law, and again in Unit IV.

    Open with what "source" means and Salmond's tree. Then legislation, precedent and custom, each with definition, kinds, one comparison and the Indian position. Then two lines each on juristic writing, equity and agreement. End by noting that the relative importance of sources has shifted: custom dominated early societies, legislation dominates modern ones, and precedent fills the gaps.
