Answer skeletons

Open one, then try writing the answer from the skeleton alone. If you get stuck on a step, that's the part of the chapter to reread.

Define jurisprudence. Discuss its nature and scope.
  1. Introduction. Etymology: juris + prudentia = knowledge of law. Say it is hard to define because "law" itself is disputed. Every definition reflects the jurist's idea of law.
  2. Definitions, grouped. Ancient: Ulpian. Analytical: Austin, Holland, Salmond (one-line criticism each). Modern: Paton, Stone, Dias & Hughes. Mention the wide → narrow → wide pattern.
  3. Nature. Uncodified; general not particular; both "is" and "ought" (Bentham); abstract yet practical; interdisciplinary; dynamic. Then the science debate in four lines, concluding it is a social science.
  4. Scope. Austin's positive-law limit and general/particular division, with Holland's criticism. Salmond's generic/specific sense; draw the tree. Modern view: scope cannot be confined; list the main areas and the schools.
  5. Utility. Two lines, with one case (Maneka Gandhi or ADM Jabalpur).
  6. Conclusion. Offer your working definition: the systematic study of the general principles, concepts and theories of law, covering what law is, how it develops, what it ought to be, and how it works in society.

Length guide: give definitions and scope the most space; nature can be crisper.

Is jurisprudence a science?
  1. Define science broadly: systematic, organised knowledge built from observation and reasoning, yielding general principles.
  2. Jurists who call it a science: Austin, Holland ("formal science"), Salmond, Gray, Pound.
  3. Arguments for: systematic; draws general principles from many systems; uses observation, classification and comparison.
  4. Arguments against: no universal laws; rules vary by place and time; no experiments; value judgments; unreliable predictions.
  5. Resolve it: scientia means knowledge; older usage. Not an exact science, but a science in the broad sense.
  6. Conclusion: it is best described as a social science.
Austin's general and particular jurisprudence. Is the division valid?
  1. Austin's starting point: jurisprudence is the philosophy of positive law; what's excluded (positive morality, divine law, international law).
  2. General jurisprudence: principles, notions and distinctions common to all mature systems. Example: concept of a right.
  3. Particular jurisprudence: any actual system or part of it. Example: Indian law of contract.
  4. Draw a two-column table comparing them.
  5. Criticism: Holland (only general can be a science); "mature systems" means mainly Roman and English law; the line blurs in practice; the positive-law fence is too narrow.
  6. Conclusion: useful as a starting distinction, but too rigid; modern jurisprudence does not respect either boundary.
"Jurisprudence is the eye of law." Discuss.
  1. Unpack the metaphor: an eye lets a body see itself and its surroundings.
  2. Inward vision: basic concepts (rights, duties, possession, personality), validity, sources of law.
  3. Outward vision: law in society; Stone's "lawyer's extraversion"; Pound's social engineering.
  4. Seeing what ought to be: Bentham's censorial jurisprudence guides reform.
  5. Show it working: Maneka Gandhi (fairness read into Article 21); ADM Jabalpur and its overruling in Puttaswamy.
  6. Conclusion: without jurisprudence, law operates but blindly.
What are the sources of law? Discuss Salmond's classification.
  1. Meaning: "source" can mean the origin of a rule's authority, its content, or its causes. A source is wherever a rule gets its force or its content.
  2. Salmond's tree (draw it): formal (the will of the State) and material; material splits into legal (legislation, precedent, custom, agreement: authoritative) and historical (writings, foreign judgments: unauthoritative). Legal sources are the "gates" of the law.
  3. Criticism: Allen (the "will of the State" explains little; custom predates the State); the formal/material line blurs. Keeton's alternative: binding and persuasive sources.
  4. Each main source in a paragraph: legislation (Salmond's definition; supreme/subordinate), precedent (ratio, stare decisis, Article 141), custom (Salmond's definition; requisites).
  5. Other sources: juristic writings (Mitakshara, Dayabhaga), equity (Maine's three agencies), agreement.
  6. Conclusion: custom dominated early law, legislation dominates modern law, precedent fills the gaps; all three still operate together in India.
The doctrine of precedent: ratio, obiter and the Indian position.
  1. Meaning: a judicial decision followed in later similar cases; like cases decided alike; certainty and equality.
  2. Kinds: declaratory and original; authoritative and persuasive; absolutely and conditionally authoritative (Salmond).
  3. Ratio and obiter: two-column table. Wambaugh's reversal test; Goodhart's material facts test.
  4. Stare decisis in England: London Tramways (1898), Young v. Bristol Aeroplane (1944), Practice Statement (1966).
  5. India: Article 141 (and 144); SC not bound by itself (Bengal Immunity, 1955); larger bench binds smaller (Dawoodi Bohra, 2005); prospective overruling (Golaknath, 1967); High Courts bind courts below them, persuasive elsewhere; SC obiter followed by lower courts.
  6. Merits and demerits in two lines each, then compare with legislation (Salmond's five points).
Define custom. What are the essentials of a valid custom?
  1. Definitions: Salmond (principles that commended themselves to the national conscience as principles of justice and public utility); Keeton's longer definition in one line.
  2. Is custom law? Austin (only when adopted by the sovereign), Savigny (truest law, from the Volksgeist), middle view (law, recognised by courts).
  3. Kinds: legal (local, general) and conventional (usage). In India, family and community customs too.
  4. Essentials, one line each: antiquity, continuity, peaceful enjoyment, certainty, reasonableness, consistency, conformity with statute, not against morality or public policy, obligatory force.
  5. Indian law: Collector of Madura (1868: clear proof of usage outweighs the written text); HMA s. 3(a) definition; Ujagar Singh v. Jeo (1959: proof and judicial notice).
  6. Close: custom versus prescription, and the shrinking role of custom as legislation grows.
Trace the development of natural law.
  1. Meaning and features: higher law discovered by reason; universal, unchanging (classically), a standard for judging positive law.
  2. Greek: Sophists (convention), Socrates, Aristotle (natural versus conventional justice), Stoics (live according to reason).
  3. Roman: Cicero's "true law is right reason"; jus civile, jus gentium, jus naturale; praetors developing the law.
  4. Medieval: Augustine; Aquinas's four laws (draw the ladder); unjust law a perversion of law.
  5. Renaissance: Grotius (secular, international law); Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau in a table.
  6. Decline: Bentham ("nonsense upon stilts"), Austin, Savigny, Hume's is/ought, scientific spirit, vagueness.
  7. Revival: Nazi law and Nuremberg, Radbruch, human rights; Stammler (variable content), Fuller (eight principles), Hart's minimum content, Finnis (seven basic goods).
  8. Conclusion: natural law changes its content with each age, but the demand that law be just keeps returning.
Natural law in India and the principles of natural justice.
  1. Indian tradition: dharma bound even the king; rita as the cosmic order.
  2. Article 21, from positivism to natural law: A.K. Gopalan (1950) versus Maneka Gandhi (1978): procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.
  3. Higher-law limits: Golaknath (fundamental rights as natural rights), Kesavananda Bharati (basic structure).
  4. Rights before the Constitution: Khanna J.'s dissent in ADM Jabalpur (1976), vindicated in Puttaswamy (2017).
  5. Natural justice: nemo judex in causa sua (A.K. Kraipak, 1969), audi alteram partem (Maneka Gandhi), reasoned decisions; exceptions (Tulsiram Patel, 1985).
  6. Conclusion: a positive Constitution, read through natural law values.

The Gopalan-to-Maneka contrast is the single most useful paragraph here. Learn it cold.

Small things that earn marks

  • Name the jurist every time. "Austin defined jurisprudence as…" beats "jurisprudence is defined as…".
  • Underline definitions, or set them on their own line. Examiners skim for them.
  • Pair every definition with a criticism. One line is enough. It shows judgment.
  • Draw something. Salmond's tree and the general/particular table each take a minute and stand out on the page.
  • Use one Indian case. Maneka Gandhi in a scope or utility answer is rare and memorable.
  • End with your own synthesis, not the last definition you wrote.
  • Draw the other two diagrams too. Salmond's tree of sources and Aquinas's four laws are each a minute's work.
  • Quote one Article. Article 141 in a precedent answer, Article 21 in a natural law answer. Write out its words.

Check your edition

Definitions here follow their standard textbook wording. Phrasing differs a little between editions and between teachers' notes. If your teacher dictated a particular version, use theirs in the exam; the meaning is what matters.

A four-week plan for this topic

You have about a month for Unit I. Definition, nature and scope are the foundation; sources and natural law carry the heaviest long questions. Both matter for Unit II, where each school of thought takes a side on them.

Week 1Understand

Read the five chapters here, alongside Paranjape. Use every widget. Don't memorise yet.

Week 2Write

Write four long answers from the skeletons, timed: definition and scope, sources, custom, natural law. Compare with the chapters and patch the gaps.

Week 3Recall

Flashcards for five minutes a day. Write three short notes from memory.

Week 4Rehearse

One timed long answer every other day. Draw Salmond's two trees and Aquinas's ladder from memory until they're automatic.
