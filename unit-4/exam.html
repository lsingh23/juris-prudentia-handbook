Exam kit · Unit IV

Turning the unit into marks

9 answer skeletons · 24 flashcards · a 4-week plan

Unit IV rewards students who can compare. Examiners rarely want Rawls alone or Devlin alone; they want Rawls against Nozick, Devlin against Hart, modernism against its critics, and then an Indian case that shows the argument mattering. This page gives you the shapes to do that.

Answer skeletons

Open one, then try writing the answer from the skeleton alone. If you get stuck on a step, that's the part of the chapter to reread.

Critically examine Rawls's theory of justice. How far is it reflected in the Indian Constitution?
  1. Introduction. A Theory of Justice (1971); "justice is the first virtue of social institutions"; justice as fairness; subject is the basic structure of society; written as an alternative to utilitarianism.
  2. Original position and veil of ignorance. Hypothetical, not historical; list what the parties don't know and what they do; primary goods.
  3. Two principles (write them out): equal basic liberty; fair equality of opportunity and the difference principle.
  4. Lexical priority: liberty first, then fair opportunity, then the difference principle. Contrast with utilitarian trade-offs.
  5. Why the parties choose them: maximin; a small numerical example (two societies, compare the worst-off). Reflective equilibrium as the method.
  6. Criticism: Nozick (manna from heaven, self-ownership, end-state); Sandel (unencumbered self); Harsanyi (average utility); Sen (capabilities).
  7. India: Preamble; Art. 14, 15(4), 16(4), 38, 39, 46; N.M. Thomas (1976) substantive equality; Indra Sawhney (1992) creamy layer as difference principle; Minerva Mills (1980) balance of Parts III and IV as lexical priority.
  8. Conclusion: the Constitution is closer to Rawls than to any rival, though it never adopts a single theory.

Length guide: the two principles and lexical priority are where the marks are. Give them the most space.

Compare the theories of justice of Rawls and Nozick.
  1. Common ground: both Harvard liberals, both anti-utilitarian, both take the separateness of persons seriously.
  2. Rawls in brief: original position, two principles, difference principle, maximin.
  3. Nozick in brief: Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974); acquisition, transfer, rectification; Lockean proviso.
  4. The key contrast: patterned/end-state (Rawls) versus historical/unpatterned (Nozick).
  5. Wilt Chamberlain: D1 → voluntary transfers → D2; liberty upsets patterns; taxation as forced labour.
  6. The State: Rawls's welfare state versus Nozick's minimal, night-watchman state.
  7. Nozick on Rawls: manna from heaven; natural talents as a common asset deny self-ownership.
  8. Against Nozick: historical injustice (caste, colonialism) makes rectification vast; ignores need.
  9. Table (two columns), then India: Article 39(b) and Property Owners Association (2024) show the tension is live; reservations as both difference principle and rectification.
Aristotle's distributive and corrective justice.
  1. Source: Nicomachean Ethics, Book V. General justice (lawfulness) versus particular justice (fairness).
  2. Distributive: honours, wealth; proportionate (geometrical) equality; according to merit; the flute example; dispute over the criterion of merit.
  3. Corrective: voluntary and involuntary transactions; arithmetical equality; parties as equals; judge restores the mean.
  4. Equity: correction of law where defective owing to its universality.
  5. Relevance: public law (tax, welfare, reservations) versus private law (tort, contract); Article 14 reasonable classification (Anwar Ali Sarkar, 1952); Article 142 complete justice.
  6. Criticism: no test of merit; accepted slavery; corrective justice can freeze unjust starting points.
The utilitarian theory of justice: Bentham and Mill.
  1. Bentham: two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure; principle of utility; greatest happiness of the greatest number; each to count for one.
  2. Felicific calculus: list all seven dimensions; the rhyme.
  3. Reform: criminal law, codification, "nonsense upon stilts".
  4. Mill: quality of pleasures (Socrates dissatisfied); justice as the most important class of utilities; harm principle.
  5. Criticism: ignores distribution; sacrifices individuals; immeasurable; Mill's quality breaks hedonism; Rawls: ignores the distinction between persons.
  6. Conclusion: utility remains a good test of legislation, but fails as a complete theory of justice because it gives rights no independent weight. Indian fundamental rights reject it.
Law and morality: relationship and historical development.
  1. Meaning: morality; positive versus critical morality.
  2. Differences table: external/internal, source, sanction, certainty, scope. Bentham: same centre, different circumference.
  3. Three roles: morality as basis, test and limit of law.
  4. History: ancient fusion (dharma, Greeks, Romans); medieval Church (Aquinas); separation (Thomasius, Kant, Austin's "existence of law is one thing"); 20th-century reconnection (Radbruch, Fuller, Hart's minimum content, Dworkin).
  5. Where they meet: s. 23 Contract Act; Donoghue v. Stevenson; Riggs v. Palmer; Articles 19(2) and 25.
  6. Modern debates: one paragraph each on Hart–Devlin and Hart–Fuller.
  7. Conclusion: distinct but inseparable; the live question is which morality.
Should the law enforce morality? The Hart–Devlin debate.
  1. Frame the question: is immorality alone a sufficient reason to criminalise?
  2. Background: Mill's harm principle versus Stephen.
  3. Wolfenden (1957): recommendations; para. 61 "not the law's business".
  4. Devlin (1959): shared morality as invisible bonds; disintegration thesis and the treason analogy; reasonable man; intolerance, indignation, disgust; elastic limits. Shaw v. DPP.
  5. Hart (1963): positive versus critical morality; no evidence of disintegration (change is not destruction); moral populism; paternalism and public decency distinguished.
  6. Assessment: Feinberg's four principles in one line.
  7. India: Naz (2009), Koushal (2013), Navtej (2018), Joseph Shine (2018), Khushboo (2010). Constitutional morality as Hart's critical morality.

Draw the four-principle table if you have time. It shows you understand why the question matters, not just who said what.

The Hart–Fuller debate. Is an unjust law a law?
  1. Setting: Harvard Law Review, 1958; Hart's "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" and Fuller's "Positivism and Fidelity to Law".
  2. The grudge informer: the facts in four lines.
  3. Hart: law but iniquitous; clarity; retrospective statute as a lesser evil.
  4. Fuller: law as the enterprise of subjecting conduct to rules; inner morality; Nazi system not law; the eight desiderata (list); morality of duty and aspiration.
  5. Hart's reply: principles of efficacy, like the poisoner's art.
  6. Radbruch's formula and Hart's minimum content of natural law as middle positions.
  7. India: Maneka Gandhi (fair, just, reasonable) and ADM Jabalpur/Puttaswamy as the Indian version of the question.
"Constitutional morality must prevail over social morality."
  1. Origin: Ambedkar, 4 November 1948, quoting Grote: "not a natural sentiment… has to be cultivated".
  2. Meaning today: the values of the Constitution (dignity, liberty, equality, fraternity) over majoritarian morality; Articles 19(2) and 25 read accordingly.
  3. Jurisprudential link: Hart's critical versus positive morality; rejection of Devlin's reasonable man.
  4. Cases in order: Naz (2009) → Koushal (2013, "minuscule fraction") → NALSA (2014) → Puttaswamy (2017) → Navtej (2018) → Joseph Shine (2018) → IYLA (2018, 4:1).
  5. Critique: vagueness; counter-majoritarian; Indu Malhotra J.'s dissent on religious pluralism; reference in Kantaru Rajeevaru (2019).
  6. Conclusion: a necessary safeguard for minorities, but it needs careful limits to stay principled.

The Naz-to-Navtej sequence is the single most useful paragraph in the unit. Learn the years cold.

Modernism and postmodernism in law; relevance to Indian legal pluralism.
  1. Define both as moods, not schools.
  2. Modernism: Enlightenment (Kant's "sapere aude"); reason, universality, progress, the individual, foundations.
  3. Modern law: codification (Code civil 1804; IPC 1860); positivism as modernist (Austin, Kelsen, Hart); Weber's legal-rational authority; Galanter's features.
  4. Postmodernism: Lyotard (metanarratives, little narratives); Foucault (power/knowledge, Panopticon, disciplinary power); Derrida (deconstruction, binaries, "Force of Law").
  5. Movements: legal pluralism (Griffiths); CLS (indeterminacy, law is politics); feminist jurisprudence (Olsen, Gilligan, MacKinnon).
  6. Criticism: relativism; self-refutation; Habermas; nihilism; Sokal.
  7. India: personal laws; Art. 13 custom; Art. 371A; Art. 44 and the UCC debate (Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal, Shayara Bano; Law Commission 2018); Narasu Appa Mali; Criminal Tribes Act as power/knowledge; NALSA.
  8. Conclusion: postmodernism is a method of critique; Indian pluralism makes it practical.

Small things that earn marks

  • Name the book and year. "Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971)" signals that you know the source, not just the idea.
  • Write the two principles out in full. Paraphrase loses marks on Rawls. The same goes for Wolfenden's "not the law's business".
  • Compare, don't list. A two-column Rawls/Nozick or Hart/Devlin table takes two minutes and stands out on the page.
  • Use a number. A tiny maximin example (Society A: worst-off 20; Society B: worst-off 24) shows you understand the difference principle.
  • Draw the four-principle table (harm, offence, paternalism, moralism) in any enforcement-of-morals answer.
  • Always end in India. Articles 38 and 39 for justice; Navtej and Joseph Shine for morality; Article 44 and 371A for pluralism.
  • Give both sides before your verdict. Every Unit IV topic is a debate. Examiners reward a reasoned conclusion, not a slogan.
  • Get the years right. 1957 Wolfenden, 1958 Hart–Fuller, 1959 Devlin, 1963 Hart, 1971 Rawls, 1974 Nozick, 1979 Lyotard.

Check your edition

Quotations here follow standard editions and judgments. Wording varies a little between translations (especially Aristotle and the French postmodernists) and between teachers' notes. If your teacher dictated a version, use theirs; the meaning is what matters.

A four-week plan for this unit

You have about a month for Unit IV. Justice is the heaviest topic; law and morality carries the most Indian case law; modernism is mostly short notes.

Week 1Understand

Read the three chapters here alongside Paranjape. Use every widget, especially the veil of ignorance and the Hart–Devlin comparison. Don't memorise yet.

Week 2Write

Four timed long answers from the skeletons: Rawls, Rawls versus Nozick, Hart–Devlin, constitutional morality. Patch the gaps.

Week 3Recall

Flashcards five minutes a day. Write short notes on the veil, felicific calculus, Wolfenden, Fuller's eight, Lyotard and Foucault from memory.

Week 4Rehearse

One timed answer every other day. Draw the four-theory comparison table and the Naz-to-Navtej timeline from memory until they're automatic.
