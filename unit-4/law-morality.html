Law and morality: where the State should stop

About 26 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

On 11 December 2013 the Supreme Court restored Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, saying that only a "minuscule fraction of the country's population" was affected and that change was for Parliament. Five years later, on 6 September 2018, a Constitution Bench overruled that decision and held that the law could not criminalise consensual sexual relations between adults. One question separated the two judgments: whose morality should the law enforce?

The relation between law and morality is one of the oldest themes in jurisprudence and one of the most examined. This chapter takes it in three moves: what the relationship is and how it has changed through history; the two great modern debates, Hart–Devlin on enforcing morals and Hart–Fuller on whether wicked law is law; and the Indian answer, built around constitutional morality.

Law and morals: same centre, different circumference

Morality is the body of standards of right and wrong conduct that a person or a society accepts. Jurists distinguish positive morality, the morality actually accepted by a given society, from critical (ideal) morality, the standards by which actual institutions, including positive morality itself, are judged. Keep that distinction ready; Hart's whole argument against Devlin depends on it.

Law and morals overlap heavily. Murder, theft, fraud and cruelty are both immoral and illegal. But they are not the same, and the classic differences are a guaranteed short question:

BasisLawMorality
ConcernExternal conduct: what you doInner motive as well: why you do it (Kant's legality versus morality)
SourceThe State: legislature, courts, recognised customConscience, religion, society, reason
SanctionPhysical and organised: fine, imprisonment, damagesSocial disapproval or a guilty conscience
CertaintyDefinite, written, uniform within a territoryVaries between people, groups and ages
ScopeNarrower: only what is socially necessary and enforceableWider: covers ingratitude, lying, unkindness
Some rulesMorally neutral rules are common (drive on the left, file returns by 31 July)Some moral duties are never made legal (charity, gratitude)

Bentham caught the relationship in one image: legislation has the same centre with morals, but not the same circumference. Law and morals aim at the same thing, the good of society, but morality covers a wider area, and law covers only the part that can usefully be enforced.

A traditional way to organise the topic (used in many Indian textbooks) looks at morality in three roles:

Basis of lawMuch of law grew out of moral rules: the criminal law, the law of contract (promises), the neighbour principle in tort, equity and natural justice.
Test of lawMorality as a standard for judging law: the natural law tradition, and modern constitutional review against rights and dignity.
Limit of lawMorality marks what law should not attempt: it cannot make people virtuous, and some private matters are "not the law's business".

Where they meet in practice

  • Contract. Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872: an agreement whose object or consideration is "immoral, or opposed to public policy" is void.
  • Tort. In Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932), Lord Atkin built the modern law of negligence on the biblical rule to love your neighbour: in law, you must not injure your neighbour.
  • Equity and interpretation. In Riggs v. Palmer (New York, 1889) a grandson who murdered his grandfather could not inherit under the will, because no one may profit from his own wrong. Dworkin made this his leading example of moral principles inside law.
  • The Constitution. Articles 19(2) and 25 allow restrictions in the interests of "decency or morality" and "morality". The question the Indian cases had to answer is: which morality?

Historical development

The relationship has swung from near identity, to sharp separation, to a qualified reconnection.

  1. AncientNo clear line · law, morals and religion fused

    In ancient India, dharma covered religious duty, morality and law alike; the Smritis mix ritual rules with rules of contract and crime. The Greeks and Romans grounded law in natural justice and right reason (Aristotle, the Stoics, Cicero).

  2. MedievalMorality as the basis of law · the Church

    Christian theology made law a branch of morality. For Aquinas, human law that conflicts with natural law is a perversion of law. The Church claimed authority to judge rulers by moral standards.

  3. 17th–19th c.The separation · Thomasius, Kant, Austin

    Thomasius and Kant distinguished law (external conduct, enforceable) from morality (inner duty). The positivists completed the separation: Austin insisted that the existence of law is one thing, its merit another. Kelsen later made law "pure" of all morality.

  4. 20th c.Reconnection · after Nuremberg

    Nazi law forced the question back open. Radbruch held that intolerably unjust statutes are not law; Fuller found an inner morality of law; Hart, though a positivist, conceded a minimum content of natural law; Dworkin argued that moral principles are part of law itself.

Austin's statement of the separation is the sentence to quote for the positivist side:

The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another. Whether it be or be not is one enquiry; whether it be or be not conformable to an assumed standard, is a different enquiry.

John Austin, The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, 1832, Lecture V

Positivist and natural law views

Behind every debate in this chapter is the same disagreement you met in Units I and II.

Positivist view

Law and morality are conceptually separate. A rule is law if it is validly made, whatever its moral content. Morality may influence law and may be a reason to disobey it, but it does not decide whether it is law. Bentham, Austin, Kelsen, Hart.

Natural law view

Law and morality are necessarily connected. A grossly unjust rule lacks the full character of law (Aquinas, Radbruch), or law has a built-in morality (Fuller), or legal reasoning necessarily involves moral principles (Dworkin, Finnis).

Two refinements earn marks. First, Hart's minimum content of natural law (The Concept of Law, 1961): given five truisms about human beings (vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, and limited understanding and strength of will), every workable legal system must contain rules protecting persons, property and promises. That is a connection between law and morality, but a contingent, factual one, not a conceptual one. Second, modern positivism splits into inclusive positivists, who accept that a legal system may make moral tests part of legal validity (as a Bill of Rights does), and exclusive positivists (Joseph Raz), who say it cannot.

Common mix-up

Positivists do not say that law and morality have nothing to do with each other, or that unjust laws must be obeyed. Hart says precisely the opposite: separating the questions lets us say clearly "this is law, but it is too wicked to obey". The separation thesis is about identifying law, not about whether law should be moral.

The Hart–Devlin debate: should law enforce morality?

The question is narrow and precise: is the fact that conduct is, by common standards, immoral, enough to justify making it a crime?

The background: Mill and Stephen

The debate had a nineteenth-century rehearsal. J.S. Mill, in On Liberty (1859), stated the harm principle: "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." The judge James Fitzjames Stephen replied in Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (1873) that the criminal law rightly expresses society's moral hatred of vice.

The Wolfenden Report, 1957

In 1954 the British government appointed a committee under Sir John Wolfenden to examine the law on homosexual offences and prostitution. Its report (1957) recommended that homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a crime, and that prostitution itself should remain lawful while street solicitation was penalised (done by the Street Offences Act, 1959). Its reasoning is the key passage:

Unless a deliberate attempt is to be made by society, acting through the agency of the law, to equate the sphere of crime with that of sin, there must remain a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business.

Report of the Committee on Homosexual Offences and Prostitution (Wolfenden Report), 1957, para. 61

The function of criminal law, said the committee, is to preserve public order and decency, and to protect citizens from what is offensive or injurious, especially the young and vulnerable. It is not to intervene in private lives or to enforce any particular pattern of behaviour further than necessary for those purposes.

Devlin: society may protect its morality

Lord Patrick Devlin, a Law Lord, replied in the Maccabaean Lecture of 1959, published with later essays as The Enforcement of Morals (1965). His argument:

  • A society is a community of ideas. It is held together by a shared morality, the "invisible bonds of common thought". Without it, society would disintegrate.
  • The disintegration thesis. Because shared morality is essential to society's existence, society may use the law to protect it, just as it uses the law of treason to protect its government. "The suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversive activities."
  • No private sphere in principle. It is not possible to set theoretical limits to the State's power to legislate against immorality.
  • The test is the ordinary person's feeling. Morality for this purpose is the judgment of the "reasonable man", the "man in the jury box" or "man in the Clapham omnibus". Where conduct provokes genuine feelings of intolerance, indignation and disgust, that is a sign it threatens society.
  • Limits in practice. Devlin offered elastic principles: maximum tolerance of individual freedom consistent with the integrity of society; respect for privacy as far as possible; the law should be slow to act because moral limits of tolerance shift; and law sets a minimum standard, not a maximum.

The English courts seemed to agree. In Shaw v. DPP (1962), the "Ladies' Directory" case, the House of Lords upheld a conviction for "conspiracy to corrupt public morals", and Viscount Simonds said the courts retained a residual power to guard the moral welfare of the State.

Hart: liberty against legal moralism

H.L.A. Hart replied in Law, Liberty and Morality (1963). His main points:

  • Positive versus critical morality. Devlin treats the morality a society happens to have as if it were the morality it ought to have. The real question is a critical one: is it morally justified to enforce positive morality?
  • No evidence for disintegration. There is no historical evidence that private deviation from sexual morality destroys societies. Devlin's argument confuses change in a society's morality with the destruction of society; on his logic, any society that changed its morals would "cease to exist".
  • Feelings are not reasons. Intolerance, indignation and disgust are not a reliable guide to what should be punished; they have supported persecution throughout history. Hart called the danger "moral populism", the idea that the majority has a moral right to dictate how all should live.
  • The cost of punishment. Criminal punishment of private conduct inflicts real suffering, on the offender and through intrusive policing, for no corresponding harm.
  • Paternalism is different. Hart accepted some legal paternalism (protecting people from harming themselves, as with drugs or the rule that consent is no defence to murder). That is protection of the person, not enforcement of morality as such.
  • Offence to public decency is different. Bigamy or public indecency may be punished as public nuisances or offences against public feeling, not because they are immoral in private.

Exam lens

Most students write Hart–Devlin as two lists. Better: frame it as one question (is immorality alone a sufficient reason to criminalise?), give Wolfenden's "not the law's business", Devlin's disintegration thesis and his three feelings, then Hart's four replies, and end with India: Navtej Singh Johar (2018) is Hart and Wolfenden vindicated through constitutional morality.

The Hart–Fuller debate: is wicked law still law?

The second debate asks a different question. Not "should law enforce morality?" but "does law have to be moral to count as law at all?"

It took place in volume 71 of the Harvard Law Review (1958). Hart, in "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (his Holmes Lecture, delivered in 1957), defended the positivist separation thesis. Lon Fuller replied in the same issue with "Positivism and Fidelity to Law: A Reply to Professor Hart". The test case was Nazi law, and in particular the "grudge informer" cases decided by German courts after the war.

Try itThe grudge informerYou are the post-war court. What do you do?

In 1944 a German woman, wishing to be rid of her husband, reported to the authorities that he had made remarks critical of Hitler while home on leave. Under Nazi statutes making such remarks an offence, he was tried and sentenced to death, though the sentence was not carried out and he was sent to the front. In 1949 the wife was prosecuted for illegally depriving her husband of his liberty. Her defence: she had only reported a crime under the law then in force.

Hart's position

Hart insisted on separating what law is from what it ought to be. The Nazi statutes were law, wicked law. Calling them "not law" is confused and dangerous: confused, because it hides a real moral choice behind a definition; dangerous, because it encourages the idea that whatever is law must be obeyed. Clarity requires saying "this is law, but it is too iniquitous to be applied or obeyed". If the informer is to be punished, it should be by an openly retrospective statute, choosing frankly between two evils: leaving her unpunished, or sacrificing the principle against retroactive punishment.

Fuller's position: the inner morality of law

Fuller argued that law is not just any order backed by power; it is "the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules". That enterprise has an inner morality, and the Nazi regime abandoned it: it used secret laws, retroactive statutes, and ignored its own rules whenever convenient. A system that degenerate does not deserve the name of law, and fidelity to law cannot be owed to it. In The Morality of Law (1964), through the parable of King Rex (which you met in Unit I), he set out the eight desiderata of legality:

  • Generality
  • Promulgation
  • Non-retroactivity
  • Clarity
  • Non-contradiction
  • Possibility of compliance
  • Constancy over time
  • Congruence of official action with declared rule

Total failure in any one of these "does not simply result in a bad system of law; it results in something that is not properly called a legal system at all". Fuller also distinguished the morality of duty (the basic rules without which ordered society is impossible) from the morality of aspiration (the pursuit of excellence). Law can enforce only the first.

Hart's reply and the verdict

Hart answered, in reviewing The Morality of Law, that Fuller's eight principles are principles of efficacy, not morality: they are like the principles of good carpentry or, in his sharpest line, of poisoning. A skilful poisoner also follows rules of craft. A wicked regime could comply with all eight and still be wicked. Fuller replied that, in practice, governments that respect publicity and generality find it harder to do great evil, because evil prefers the dark.

Neither side "won". But the debate fixed the modern terms of the question, and it explains why Indian constitutional law, with its demand that laws be fair, just and reasonable (Maneka Gandhi, 1978), looks more Fullerian than Austinian.

Sidenote

Hart relied on a published report that the post-war court had declared the Nazi statute void. Later research showed the court had actually convicted the woman on the ground that she had acted contrary to "the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings", without squarely holding the statute invalid. The philosophical argument is unaffected, but it's a nice point to mention.

The enforcement of morality: four principles

Out of these debates came a vocabulary for why the State may criminalise conduct. Joel Feinberg's four-volume The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law (1984–88) is the standard source. Use these terms precisely.

Indian courts have moved steadily away from legal moralism towards the harm principle:

  • Obscenity. Ranjit D. Udeshi v. State of Maharashtra (1965) applied the old English Hicklin test (tendency to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences). Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal (2014) replaced it with a contemporary community standards test.
  • Live-in relationships. In S. Khushboo v. Kanniammal (2010) the Court quashed criminal complaints against an actress who had spoken in favour of pre-marital sex, holding that conduct some regard as immoral is not thereby an offence: morality and criminality are not co-extensive.
  • Dance bars. In Indian Hotel and Restaurant Association v. State of Maharashtra (2019) the Court struck down several restrictive licensing conditions as arbitrary and disproportionate: a subjective notion of social morality could not justify them.

India: constitutional morality versus popular morality

The Indian Supreme Court's answer to Devlin is a concept with a surprising origin. On 4 November 1948, introducing the Draft Constitution, Dr B.R. Ambedkar quoted the historian Grote:

Constitutional morality is not a natural sentiment. It has to be cultivated. We must realise that our people have yet to learn it. Democracy in India is only a top-dressing on an Indian soil, which is essentially undemocratic.

Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 4 November 1948

Ambedkar meant respect for constitutional forms and restraint by those in power. The Supreme Court has since given the phrase a fuller meaning: the morality of the Constitution's own values (dignity, liberty, equality, non-discrimination and fraternity), which prevails over popular or majoritarian morality. When Articles 19(2) and 25 speak of "morality", they now mean constitutional morality, not the feelings of the "man in the Clapham omnibus". That is a direct rejection of Devlin's test.

The reasoning
Which side of the debate

Is constitutional morality itself a problem?

The doctrine has its critics, and a balanced answer mentions them:

  • Vagueness. If "constitutional morality" means whatever the judges find in the Constitution's values, it can become a blank cheque. A serving Attorney General publicly warned in 2018 that it could turn into a "dangerous weapon".
  • Counter-majoritarian worry. Unelected judges overriding the moral views of the community and of Parliament raises the democratic objection, the mirror image of Devlin's.
  • Religion and pluralism. Indu Malhotra J.'s dissent in Sabarimala argued that, in a plural society, constitutional morality also requires respecting the freedom of religious denominations to manage their own affairs. The reference to a larger bench shows the question is unsettled.

The defence: constitutional morality is not the judges' private morality but the critical morality the Constitution itself adopts. It is exactly Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality, written into a constitutional text. Courts enforce it to protect individuals, including minorities, from the majority's positive morality, which is what fundamental rights are for.

How to write it up

Positivist and natural law views, with Hart's minimum content. Hart–Devlin: Mill and Stephen, Wolfenden (para. 61), Devlin's disintegration thesis, Hart's replies. Hart–Fuller: the grudge informer, retrospective statute versus inner morality, the eight desiderata, the poisoner reply. Enforcement: harm, offence, paternalism, moralism. India: Ambedkar on constitutional morality, Naz, Koushal, Navtej, Joseph Shine, Sabarimala. Conclude: law and morality are distinct but inseparable; the real question is which morality the law may enforce.
