Modernism, postmodernism and the law

About 24 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1871 the colonial government passed the Criminal Tribes Act. It declared whole communities "addicted to the systematic commission of non-bailable offences", registered them, fingerprinted them and confined them to settlements. It was drafted in the clean, general language of a modern statute, enforced by trained officials, and justified by the science of the day. It was also a catastrophe for the people it named.

That Act sits on the fault line this chapter is about. Modernism promised that law, built on reason, would be universal, neutral and a tool of progress. Postmodernism asks who wrote the rules, whose "reason" it was, and whom the neutral categories quietly left out. You don't need to choose a side to pass the exam. You do need to explain both clearly, and to show why the argument matters for a country as plural as India.

Two moods, not two schools

A word of warning first. Neither "modernism" nor "postmodernism" is a school of jurisprudence in the way the analytical or historical schools are. They are broad intellectual moods that run across philosophy, art, architecture and social theory, and law is one place where they show up.

Modernism

Confidence in reason. There is truth to be found, universal principles to be stated, and progress to be made by applying them. Law can be systematic, general and neutral.

Postmodernism

Suspicion of that confidence. "Truth", "reason" and "neutrality" are products of language, power and history. Law's universal claims hide particular interests and exclude other voices.

Modernism asks "what is the right answer?" Postmodernism asks "who gets to decide what counts as an answer?"

Roughly, modernity runs from the Enlightenment of the 17th and 18th centuries to the middle of the 20th. Postmodern thought took shape in France in the 1960s and 1970s and reached legal scholarship, mainly through American law schools, in the 1980s.

The modernist project

Modernism is rooted in the Enlightenment, the movement that put human reason, not tradition or revelation, at the centre of knowledge. Immanuel Kant's essay What is Enlightenment? (1784) gave it a motto:

Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity. Sapere aude! Have the courage to use your own understanding.

Immanuel Kant, An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?, 1784

From that starting point come the beliefs that define the modern outlook:

  • Rationality. Reason can discover truth, in science and in ethics. Descartes's method of doubt, Newton's laws and Kant's moral law all share this faith.
  • Universality. What is true and right is true and right everywhere. Human rights belong to "all men", not to Frenchmen or Englishmen.
  • Progress. History moves forward. Applying reason will steadily free humanity from ignorance, poverty and tyranny.
  • The autonomous individual. The rational, self-governing person is the basic unit of society and the bearer of rights.
  • Secularism and the separation of spheres. Law is separated from religion, the State from the Church, fact from value.
  • Foundations. Knowledge can be built on secure foundations, from which everything else follows.

How modernism shaped law

Modern law is so familiar that we forget it had to be invented. Its features are the Enlightenment's beliefs translated into legal form.

Codification

If law is rational, it can be written down completely and systematically, in a code that any citizen can read. The great example is the French Code civil of 1804, the Napoleonic Code, followed by the German BGB (1900). Bentham, who coined the word "codification", spent decades urging codes on governments across the world.

India was a laboratory of codification. The first Law Commission under Macaulay drafted the Indian Penal Code (enacted 1860), followed by the Code of Civil Procedure, the Code of Criminal Procedure, the Indian Contract Act and the Indian Evidence Act (both 1872), and many more. The modernist belief that one uniform, territorial code could govern a vast and diverse population is written into all of them, and into their 2023 successors, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam.

Positivism as a modernist theory

Legal positivism is modernism's own theory of law. It shares every modernist instinct:

Modernist beliefPositivist expression
Reason and scienceAustin wants a "science of positive law"; Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law (1934) purifies law of ethics, politics and sociology to make it a science
Fact/value splitSeparation of law as it is from law as it ought to be (Bentham, Austin, Hart)
FoundationsEvery valid norm traces back to a single source: the sovereign (Austin), the Grundnorm (Kelsen), the rule of recognition (Hart)
System and unityLaw as a closed, hierarchical, internally consistent system of rules
UniversalityGeneral jurisprudence: concepts common to all mature legal systems
The StateLaw is State law; other normative orders (custom, religion) are law only if the State recognises them

Natural law in its Enlightenment form (Locke, Kant, the declarations of rights) is modernist too: it trusts reason to find universal rights. Modernism is not a single theory of law but the shared ground that positivism and secular natural law both stand on.

Weber and Galanter

The sociologist Max Weber described modern authority as legal-rational: people obey because rules have been enacted by correct procedure, not because of tradition or a leader's charisma. Modern law, for Weber, is formally rational: general rules applied by trained officials through logical reasoning. He also warned of its cost: the "iron cage" of bureaucracy and a disenchanted world.

The American scholar Marc Galanter, writing about India, listed the features of "modern law": rules that are uniform and territorial, universalistic in application, administered by a hierarchy of courts and a bureaucracy, rational, run by professional lawyers, amendable by deliberate change, and monopolised by the State. Every one of these is a modernist ideal. Galanter's question was what happens when such a system is placed over a society that organises itself very differently.

Exam lens

Examiners like the sentence "legal positivism is the jurisprudence of modernism". Back it with two points: Kelsen's purity and Hart's rule of recognition (a single foundation), and codification (law as a complete, rational system). Then you've earned the right to bring in the postmodern critique.

The postmodern challenge

By the mid-twentieth century, the promise of modernity looked tarnished. The most "rational" and bureaucratic States had produced world wars, the Holocaust, colonial domination and the atomic bomb. Postmodern thinkers did not reject reason outright; they asked whether reason's claims to universality and neutrality were what they seemed. Their main moves:

  • Anti-foundationalism. There is no neutral, secure foundation for knowledge or law.
  • The role of language. Meaning is not fixed; it is produced by language and is always open to interpretation.
  • Power. What counts as "knowledge" and "truth" is bound up with power.
  • Difference and plurality. In place of one universal story, many local, partial ones; attention to voices that the universal story excluded.
  • The decentred subject. The autonomous rational individual is itself a construction of discourse, not a given.

Lyotard: incredulity towards metanarratives

Jean-François Lyotard (1924–1998) wrote The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge (1979) for the government of Quebec, and it gave postmodernism its most quoted definition:

Simplifying to the extreme, I define postmodern as incredulity toward metanarratives.

Jean-François Lyotard, The Postmodern Condition, 1979

A metanarrative (grand narrative) is a big story that claims to explain and legitimate everything else: the Enlightenment story of the progressive emancipation of humanity through reason, the Marxist story of class struggle ending in communism, the Hegelian story of Spirit realising itself in history. Modern knowledge justified itself by appeal to such stories. Lyotard says we no longer believe them. What remains are little narratives (petits récits): local, plural, provisional, each with its own rules, like Wittgenstein's language games, none of which can claim to judge all the others.

For law, this is a direct challenge. The rule of law, human rights and constitutional progress are all told as metanarratives. If we are incredulous towards them, law's claim to universal legitimacy must be rethought. Lyotard also warned that, without grand narratives, knowledge would be legitimated by performativity: whatever works most efficiently. Law justified only by efficiency is a worrying prospect.

Foucault: power and knowledge

Michel Foucault (1926–1984) studied how modern institutions (the prison, the asylum, the clinic, the school) produce and control people. In Discipline and Punish (1975) he contrasts the public torture of the old regime with the modern prison, which looks more humane but works through constant surveillance, timetables, examinations and records.

His symbol for this is the Panopticon, Bentham's design for a circular prison in which a single guard in a central tower can watch every cell without being seen. Because prisoners never know whether they are being watched, they discipline themselves. For Foucault, modern society as a whole works like this. Power is not just a sovereign who commands; it is everywhere, in the norms and categories that shape how we see ourselves.

The key idea is power/knowledge (pouvoir/savoir): power and knowledge produce each other. Criminology, psychiatry and statistics create categories ("the delinquent", "the habitual offender", "the criminal tribe") which then become objects of control. Power is also productive, not just repressive: it creates identities, not only prohibitions. In The History of Sexuality (1976) Foucault adds biopower: the State's management of populations through birth rates, health and sexuality.

For jurisprudence this unsettles Austin. If power works through discipline and normalisation rather than commands backed by sanctions, a theory of law that looks only at the sovereign's commands misses most of how society is actually governed. Foucault even suggested that modern society is less juridical and more disciplinary: the norm is gradually replacing the law.

Derrida: deconstruction

Jacques Derrida (1930–2004), in Of Grammatology (1967) and later work, developed deconstruction, a way of reading texts to show that they do not have the stable, single meaning they seem to claim. His famous phrase is il n'y a pas de hors-texte, "there is nothing outside the text": we never escape interpretation to reach meaning directly.

Deconstruction focuses on binary oppositions: reason/emotion, man/woman, public/private, law/violence. In each pair, Western thought privileges the first term and treats the second as inferior or derivative. A deconstructive reading shows that the privileged term depends on the one it excludes, and that the hierarchy can be reversed. Derrida's coinage différance captures the idea that meaning arises from difference and is always deferred, never finally present.

His most important essay for lawyers is "Force of Law: The Mystical Foundation of Authority" (lecture at Cardozo Law School, 1989). Every legal order begins with an act of founding (a revolution, a constitution) that could not itself have been legal, since there was no law yet to authorise it. Law's authority rests on a moment of force. Derrida then distinguishes law (droit), which is always deconstructible, from justice, which is not: "Deconstruction is justice." Justice is the demand, never fully met, to respond to each singular case; law is our imperfect, calculable attempt to meet it. That is a surprisingly hopeful conclusion, and a good one to use in the exam.

Pluralism, CLS and feminist jurisprudence

Postmodern ideas reached law mostly through movements that already had a critical agenda. They share the postmodern suspicion of neutrality, but each has its own focus.

Legal pluralism

Modernist law assumes legal centralism: one State, one law, and everything else is "custom" or "religion". Legal pluralism denies this. In any society, several normative orders operate side by side: State law, religious law, customary law, the rules of a caste panchayat, a market, a corporation. John Griffiths' essay "What is Legal Pluralism?" (1986) attacked "the ideology of legal centralism"; Sally Falk Moore described "semi-autonomous social fields" that make their own rules while being partly shaped by State law. The fit with Lyotard is obvious: many little narratives instead of one grand one.

Critical Legal Studies (CLS)

The CLS movement began at a conference at the University of Wisconsin in 1977. Its leading figures include Duncan Kennedy, Roberto Mangabeira Unger and Mark Kelman. Its themes, building on American legal realism and on Marx, Gramsci and the postmodernists:

  • Indeterminacy. Legal rules and doctrines do not determine outcomes; for every principle there is a counter-principle, and judges choose.
  • Law is politics. Because rules don't decide cases, decisions reflect political and ideological choices dressed up as neutral reasoning.
  • Legitimation. Law's appearance of neutrality makes existing hierarchies (of class, race, gender) look natural and inevitable.
  • "Trashing". Taking apart legal texts to expose their contradictions and hidden assumptions, a close cousin of deconstruction.

Feminist jurisprudence

Feminist legal theory asks how law has been shaped by and for men, while claiming to be gender-neutral. Not all feminist jurisprudence is postmodern, but much of it uses postmodern tools:

  • Frances Olsen ("The Sex of Law") showed how liberal thought arranges dualisms (rational/irrational, objective/subjective, public/private) and associates law with the "male" side, a deconstructive move.
  • Carol Gilligan's In a Different Voice (1982) contrasted an ethic of justice and rights with an ethic of care, and asked why law hears only the first.
  • Catharine MacKinnon argued that the "neutral" standpoint of law is in fact the male standpoint, and that gender is a matter of dominance, not mere difference.
  • Postmodern feminists such as Judith Butler (Gender Trouble, 1990) go further and treat gender itself as performed, not natural, which challenges the binary categories the law uses.

Critical race theory (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, who coined "intersectionality" in 1989) applies the same critique to race. All of these are what Lyotard would call little narratives: voices from below insisting that the universal story did not include them.

Criticism of postmodernism

Postmodernism has many critics, and the criticisms are a reliable short question.

  • Relativism. If there is no truth, only perspectives, we cannot say that apartheid or untouchability is really wrong. That leaves the oppressed with no ground to stand on, which is ironic for a theory that claims to speak for them.
  • Self-refutation. "There are no metanarratives" is itself a sweeping claim about all of knowledge, that is, a metanarrative. The critique saws off the branch it sits on.
  • Habermas's defence of modernity. Jürgen Habermas called modernity "an unfinished project" (lecture, 1980) and answered the postmodernists in The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity (1985). The answer to the failures of reason is better reason, through open, rational discourse, not the abandonment of reason. He accused the postmodernists of a "performative contradiction": using rational argument to attack rationality.
  • Nihilism and quietism. Deconstruction can take any text apart but offers nothing to put in its place. Critics say it leads to political paralysis.
  • Obscurity. Much postmodern writing is deliberately difficult. In 1996 the physicist Alan Sokal published a nonsense article in a postmodern journal (Social Text) to show how little was being checked.
  • It needs the law it criticises. Feminist and critical race scholars themselves noted that oppressed groups need rights and rules. Patricia Williams, for example, argued that rights mean something different to people who have never had them.

The fair conclusion: postmodernism is better as a method of critique than as a theory of law. It is very good at asking whose interests a "neutral" rule serves. It is weak at telling a judge what to decide. Derrida's own line, that law is deconstructible but justice is not, is the best bridge back.

Common mix-up

Don't write that postmodernism "rejects law" or "believes in nothing". Its claim is narrower: that law's claims to neutrality, universality and determinacy are overstated. Most postmodern legal scholars want law to be more just, not abolished.

Relevance to Indian legal pluralism

If postmodernism sometimes feels like a seminar-room debate in Paris or Harvard, India makes it concrete. Indian law is one of the most plural legal orders in the world, and the modern/postmodern tension runs through it.

A plural legal order

  • Personal laws. Marriage, divorce, succession and adoption are governed by different laws for Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Parsis and others, alongside the secular Special Marriage Act, 1954.
  • Custom. Article 13(3)(a) defines "law" to include "custom or usage having the force of law". Hindu law expressly saves customs (Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, s. 3(a)).
  • Tribal and regional autonomy. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules protect tribal areas; Article 371A says no Act of Parliament on Naga religious or social practices, Naga customary law and procedure, or ownership of land, applies to Nagaland unless its Assembly so decides. Article 371G does the same for Mizoram.
  • Non-State forums. Caste and community panchayats, religious bodies and, within the State system, Lok Adalats (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987) and gram nyayalayas, which resolve disputes by compromise rather than strict rules.

Modernist pressure: uniformity

Article 44 directs the State to "endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India", the purest modernist ideal: one rational law for all. The courts have repeatedly invoked it: in Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum (1985) and Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India (1995) the Supreme Court regretted that Article 44 remained a dead letter. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) it held instantaneous triple talaq unlawful. Uttarakhand enacted the first State-level Uniform Civil Code in 2024.

Pluralist resistance: difference

Against this, pluralists argue that a single code would in practice impose the majority's norms as "neutral" ones, exactly the postmodern worry about universal categories. The courts have also, at times, left personal law outside the reach of fundamental rights: in State of Bombay v. Narasu Appa Mali (1952) the Bombay High Court held that uncodified personal laws are not "laws in force" under Article 13. The Law Commission's 2018 consultation paper on family law reform said a uniform civil code was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage", recommending reform within each personal law instead.

Foucault in India

The Criminal Tribes Act

1871 · repealed after Independence

Colonial ethnography produced the category "criminal tribe"; the law then registered, surveilled and confined the people it had classified. It is a textbook case of power/knowledge. The communities were "denotified" after repeal, but the Habitual Offenders Acts that replaced it have been criticised for continuing the stigma.

Beyond the binary

NALSA v. Union of India

2014

The Supreme Court recognised transgender persons as a "third gender" with rights under Articles 14, 15, 16, 19 and 21, and upheld the right to self-identify one's gender. The law's male/female binary was, in effect, deconstructed: the Court accepted that a category the law treated as natural was socially constructed and exclusionary.

State law versus community law

Shakti Vahini v. Union of India

2018

The Court held that khap panchayats have no authority to interfere with the marriage of consenting adults and issued directions to prevent "honour" killings. Pluralism has limits: a community's normative order cannot override constitutional rights.

Indian scholars have taken up these themes. Upendra Baxi criticised the colonial and elite character of the Indian legal system and argued for a law that listens to the "voices of suffering". Marc Galanter showed how the modern legal system displaced, but never eliminated, older indigenous forms of dispute resolution. Both point to the same conclusion: in India, a purely modernist jurisprudence that sees only State law misses most of the law people actually live under.

How to write it up

Define both as intellectual moods, not schools. Modernism: Enlightenment (Kant), reason, universality, progress, the individual; in law, codification and positivism (Austin, Kelsen, Hart), Weber's legal-rational authority. Postmodernism: Lyotard's incredulity towards metanarratives, Foucault's power/knowledge and the Panopticon, Derrida's deconstruction and "Force of Law". Then pluralism, CLS and feminist jurisprudence. Criticism (relativism, self-refutation, Habermas). India: personal laws, Art. 13 custom, Art. 371A, Art. 44 and the UCC debate, NALSA. Conclude that postmodernism is valuable as critique, and that Indian pluralism makes it practical, not merely theoretical.
