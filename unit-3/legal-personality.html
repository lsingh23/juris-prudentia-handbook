Legal personality: who counts as a person?

About 25 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

On 20 March 2017, the Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganga and the Yamuna to be legal persons, "living entities" with the rights, duties and liabilities of a living person. Three officials were appointed to act as their guardians. Four months later, the Supreme Court stayed the order. Can a river really be a person?

The question sounds strange until you notice how many non-humans the law already treats as persons. A company can own land and be sued. A Hindu idol can hold property. The Guru Granth Sahib is a juristic person. Meanwhile, for most of history, slaves were not legal persons at all. This chapter explains what "person" means in law, who gets the status, and the theories jurists have built to explain why.

What a person is in law

The word comes from the Latin persona, the mask an actor wore on the Roman stage. It came to mean the role a person plays, and then, in law, the role of being a bearer of rights and duties. That origin is a useful clue: legal personality is a role the law assigns, not a biological fact.

A person is any being whom the law regards as capable of rights and duties. Any being that is so capable is a person, whether a human being or not, and no being that is not so capable is a person, even though he be a man.

Sir John Salmond, Jurisprudence

Salmond's definition makes two points at once: non-humans can be persons, and humans can fail to be (the slave in Roman law; the "civilly dead" sanyasi in classical Hindu law, who on renouncing the world was treated as dead for purposes of property). Gray says the technical legal meaning of a person is simply "a subject of legal rights and duties". Kelsen goes further: even a human being is a person only as "the personification of a complex of legal norms".

Natural persons

Human beings, who are persons because the law recognises them as such. Personality normally begins at birth and ends at death. Minors and persons of unsound mind are full persons, though with limited capacity.

Legal (juristic, artificial, fictitious) persons

Beings, real or imaginary, other than humans, to whom the law attributes personality: companies, universities, the State, idols, trusts in some systems, and, in recent Indian cases, rivers and animals.

Salmond divides legal persons into three classes, according to what is personified:

corporationsA group or series of persons: a company, a municipality, a university, a registered trade union.
institutionsAn institution as such: a church, a hospital, a library, a temple. English law incorporates them; Hindu law personifies the idol.
funds or estatesProperty devoted to a purpose: a charitable fund, a deceased's estate, an insolvent's estate.

Legal personality matters for one practical reason above all: it lets the law treat a thing or group as a single unit that can own property, make contracts, sue and be sued, and bear liability, separately from the human beings involved.

The edges of natural personality: the unborn, the dead, animals

Salmond's chapter on persons spends time on three borderline cases. Each is a short-note favourite.

The unborn child

A child in the womb is not yet a person, but the law makes it one by anticipation for certain purposes, provided it is later born alive. This is the rule summed up as nasciturus pro iam nato habetur: the unborn is treated as born whenever that is for its benefit. In Indian law:

  • Property. Under section 13 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, property may be transferred for the benefit of an unborn person, subject to a prior interest, and the interest must extend to the whole remaining interest. Under section 20, such a person acquires a vested interest on birth.
  • Succession. Section 20 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956: a child in the womb at the intestate's death, later born alive, inherits as if born before the death.
  • Criminal law. Causing miscarriage, and acts done to prevent a child being born alive, are offences (sections 312–316 IPC; now sections 88–92 of the BNS, 2023). A death sentence on a pregnant woman must be postponed or commuted.
  • Limits. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 permits termination within statutory limits. The foetus's interests are protected, but it is not a full person able to override the mother's rights.

The dead

Personality ends at death: dead persons are no longer persons in the eye of the law, and they cannot own property or bear duties. But Salmond notes that the law still protects three things connected with them:

  1. The body: indignity to a corpse and trespass on burial places are offences (section 297 IPC; now section 301 BNS). In Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1995), the Supreme Court held that the right to dignity and fair treatment under Article 21 extends to a person's dead body; in Ashray Adhikar Abhiyan v. Union of India (2002) it ordered a decent burial for the homeless dead.
  2. The reputation: an imputation against a deceased person can be defamation if it would harm the person's reputation if living and hurt the family (Explanation 1 to section 499 IPC; now section 356 BNS).
  3. The last wishes: a valid will is carried out, so the dead person's intention continues to govern his property.

Strictly, these protect the interests of the living (the family, the public's sense of decency), which is how Salmond explains them without giving the dead personality.

Animals

Traditionally, animals are things, not persons. They can be owned, and their owners are liable for the harm they do. In medieval Europe, animals were sometimes tried and executed, but modern law treats this as a curiosity. Salmond's view was that the law imposes duties regarding animals (not to be cruel) but that those duties are owed to the public, not to the animal. Indian law has moved away from that view; see the cases below.

Corporations and Salomon's case

A corporation is a group or succession of persons that the law treats as a single person. Salmond distinguishes two kinds:

Corporation aggregate

A group of persons co-existing at the same time, incorporated as one: a company, a university, a municipal corporation, a co-operative society, a registered trade union (section 13, Trade Unions Act, 1926).

Corporation sole

A succession of single persons holding an office, incorporated so that the office continues: the Crown in English law, a bishop. In India: the Treasurer of Charitable Endowments, the Administrator-General, the Official Trustee.

Under section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013, from the date of incorporation a company is a body corporate with perpetual succession, able to acquire, hold and dispose of property, contract, and sue and be sued in its own name. A partnership firm in India is not a legal person: it is only a compendious name for the partners (Dulichand Laxminarayan v. CIT, 1956), though tax law treats it as a unit for assessment.

Salomon v. A. Salomon & Co. Ltd (1897)

This House of Lords case is the foundation of company law and the most important case in this chapter. Aron Salomon, a leather and boot manufacturer, turned his business into a company. He held 20,001 of its 20,007 shares; his wife and five children held one each. The company paid him partly in debentures (secured loans) worth £10,000. When the company went into liquidation, unsecured creditors argued that the company was just Salomon under another name, and that he should not be paid ahead of them.

The House of Lords disagreed. Once validly incorporated, the company is a different person from its members, even if one man holds almost all the shares. It was not Salomon's agent or trustee; Salomon was its secured creditor and was entitled to be paid first. As Lord Macnaghten put it, the company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers.

Separate person, separate employee

Lee v. Lee's Air Farming Ltd

1961 (Privy Council)

Lee was the controlling shareholder and sole governing director of a crop-spraying company, and also its pilot. When he was killed flying for it, his widow was entitled to workers' compensation: Lee could be an employee of a company he controlled.

Company property is not shareholders' property

Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co.

1925

Macaura sold his timber to a company he owned, but insured it in his own name. When it burned, he could not claim: he had no insurable interest in property belonging to the company.

The Indian position

Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, Bombay

1955

A shareholder is not a part-owner of the company's property. Dividends from a tea company were not "agricultural income" in the shareholder's hands, because the land belonged to the company, not to her.

Common mix-up

"Separate personality" and "limited liability" are different things. Separate personality means the company, not its members, owns its property and owes its debts. Limited liability means the members' obligation to contribute is capped at the unpaid amount on their shares. An unlimited company has separate personality without limited liability.

Lifting the corporate veil

Salomon puts a "veil" between the company and the people behind it. Courts will sometimes lift (or pierce) the veil: ignore the separate personality and look at the people in control. They do this sparingly. The usual grounds, collected in Paranjape and in LIC v. Escorts Ltd (1986), where Chinnappa Reddy J. said the veil may be lifted where a statute requires it, where fraud or improper conduct is intended, or where the company is used to evade taxes or a beneficial statute:

  • Fraud or sham: the company is a mere façade to evade an existing legal obligation.
  • Enemy character: in wartime, to find out who really controls a company.
  • Tax evasion or the avoidance of welfare legislation.
  • Statute: the Companies Act itself makes directors personally liable in some cases (fraudulent conduct of business, for example).
  • Agency or single economic unit, in limited circumstances, though courts are cautious about treating groups of companies as one.

Two constitutional cases show the veil working in the other direction. In State Trading Corporation of India v. CTO (1963), a nine-judge bench held that a company is not a citizen and cannot claim Article 19 rights. In Tata Engineering & Locomotive Co. v. State of Bihar (1965), the Court refused to lift the veil so that the company could claim them through its citizen shareholders. Later, in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970, the Bank Nationalisation case), the Court allowed shareholders to challenge a law that affected their own rights through the company.

Beyond humans and companies: idols, scripture, rivers, animals

Indian law has stretched legal personality further than almost any other system. The reasoning in each case is worth knowing, because it shows which theory the court was using.

Idol as juristic person

Pramatha Nath Mullick v. Pradyumna Kumar Mullick

1925 (Privy Council)

Family members disputed whether a family idol could be moved to another place. The Privy Council held that a Hindu idol is a juristic entity with the capacity to sue and be sued, whose interests are looked after by the shebait. The idol has a "will" of its own about its location, and since the shebaits were the disputing parties, the court appointed a disinterested next friend to represent the idol.

Idol holds property, and pays tax

Yogendra Nath Naskar v. CIT

1969

The Supreme Court held that a Hindu idol is a juristic person capable of holding property and of being taxed through its shebait. The idol is the embodiment of the founder's pious purpose.

Scripture as juristic person

Shiromani Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee v. Som Nath Dass

2000

The Supreme Court held that the Guru Granth Sahib is a juristic person and can hold property. It is revered as the living Guru, and personality is conferred to meet the needs of the faith. The Court stressed that the concept of a juristic person is elastic, and comparing it with an idol was beside the point.

Where the line was drawn

Masjid Shahid Ganj v. SGPC; M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das

1940 (Privy Council); 2019

The Privy Council held that a mosque was not a juristic person able to sue. In the Ayodhya case, the Supreme Court held that the deity Bhagwan Shri Ram Lalla Virajman is a juristic person, but that the land, Asthan Shri Ram Janam Bhumi, is not: personifying land would make it immune from the ordinary law of possession.

Rivers as living entities

Mohd. Salim v. State of Uttarakhand

2017 (Uttarakhand High Court)

The High Court declared the Ganga and Yamuna and their tributaries legal and living entities with the status of a legal person, and named the Director of the Namami Gange project, the Chief Secretary and the Advocate General of Uttarakhand as persons in loco parentis. Days later, Lalit Miglani (2017) extended this to the Gangotri and Yamunotri glaciers, forests and lakes. The Supreme Court stayed the Mohd. Salim order in July 2017 on the State's appeal; the State had argued, among other things, that it could not be made answerable for harm the rivers cause, such as floods.

Animals' rights

Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja

2014

The Supreme Court banned jallikattu and bullock-cart races. It held that animals have a right to live in a healthy environment, free from unnecessary pain, and with dignity, recognised the "five freedoms", and read the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 with Articles 21, 51A(g) and 51A(h). It did not declare animals legal persons. That step was taken by the Uttarakhand High Court in Narayan Dutt Bhatt v. Union of India (2018) and the Punjab and Haryana High Court in Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana (2019), which declared the entire animal kingdom legal entities with citizens in loco parentis.

Exam lens

Use these cases to answer the theory question. The idol cases fit the purpose theory (property dedicated to a pious purpose) and the fiction theory (personality conferred by law). The river and animal cases show legal personality as a tool: the court gives personality to something so that its interests can be represented by a guardian. That is the strongest argument that personality is a legal device, not a natural fact.

Theories of corporate personality

What is a corporation? A creation of the law, a real social being, a shorthand for its members, or a fund with a purpose? Each answer is a theory, and you need all six for a long answer. Step through them.

CompareSix theories of legal personalityPick a theory

A creation of lawA real social being

1. The fiction theory

Associated with Savigny, and followed in England by Salmond, Coke and Blackstone. Only human beings are real persons. The personality of a corporation is a fiction: the law pretends that a group is a person, for convenience. The corporation has no real will or mind of its own. Criticism: if the corporation is fictitious, so is the State; the theory struggles to explain how a fiction can commit a crime or a tort.

2. The concession theory

An offshoot of the fiction theory, associated with Savigny, Dicey and Salmond. A corporation has legal personality only because, and only to the extent that, the State concedes it. Its importance is political: it puts all associations at the mercy of the State. Hobbes compared corporations to "worms in the entrails of a natural man". Criticism: groups such as families, churches and clubs exist and act before the State recognises them.

3. The realist (organic) theory

Developed by the German jurist Otto von Gierke and introduced to English readers by F.W. Maitland. A corporation is a real person, with a real collective will (Gesamtwille) distinct from its members' wills. The law does not create it; it merely recognises an existing social reality, as it recognises a human being. Criticism: the "group will" is a metaphor, if not a mystical idea; and the theory cannot explain a corporation sole or a one-person company, where there is no group.

4. The bracket (symbolist) theory

Associated with Ihering. The members are the real holders of the rights; the corporation is only a bracket or symbol put round them for convenience, a short way of referring to the members collectively. The personality is useful but not real. Criticism: it ignores the cases where the corporation's rights and duties plainly differ from its members' (Salomon, Macaura), and it cannot explain institutions with no members, such as an idol.

5. The purpose theory

Developed by Brinz and Bekker (the theory of Zweckvermögen, "purpose property"). Only human beings can be persons. Corporate property is not owned by anyone; it is property dedicated to a purpose, and the law protects it for that purpose. Criticism: rights without a subject are hard to imagine; the theory says little about corporate duties. But it fits endowments, charitable trusts and Hindu idols remarkably well.

6. Kelsen's pure theory

Hans Kelsen dissolves the debate. There is no difference in kind between natural and legal persons: both are constructs of legal thought. A "physical person" is the personification of the norms governing one individual's behaviour; a "juristic person" is the personification of a set of norms governing a group. "Person" is just a technical tool for describing rights and duties. Criticism: it is formally neat but ignores the social reality of groups, which is exactly what the realists wanted to capture.

How to write it up

Meaning (persona; Salmond's definition); natural and legal persons; Salmond's three classes; the unborn, the dead and animals in a paragraph each. Then corporations: aggregate and sole, Salomon, the veil. Then the six theories with a criticism each, and the Indian cases as illustrations. Conclude with H.L.A. Hart's point in "Definition and Theory in Jurisprudence" (1953): we should ask how the word "person" works in legal rules, not search for a hidden entity behind it.

Corporate liability: can a company be guilty?

If a company is a person, it should be liable like one. Civil liability was accepted early: a company is liable on its contracts and vicariously liable for the torts of its servants in the course of employment. Two harder questions remain.

The ultra vires rule

In Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. v. Riche (1875), the House of Lords held that a contract beyond the objects in a company's memorandum is void, and cannot be ratified even by all the shareholders. The fiction and concession theories explain this neatly: a creature of statute can do only what its constitution allows.

Criminal liability and mens rea

A company has no body to imprison and no mind of its own. English courts answered the mind problem with the identification (alter ego) doctrine: the state of mind of those who are the company's "directing mind and will" is the company's own (Viscount Haldane in Lennard's Carrying Co. v. Asiatic Petroleum, 1915; Lord Denning's "brain and nerve centre" in H.L. Bolton v. T.J. Graham, 1957). In Tesco Supermarkets v. Nattrass (1972), a store manager was held not to be part of that directing mind, so the company escaped liability.

Companies can be prosecuted

Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement

2005

A Constitution Bench held that a company can be prosecuted even for an offence punishable with mandatory imprisonment and fine. Since it cannot be imprisoned, the court may impose a fine alone. This overruled the contrary view in Velliappa Textiles (2003).

Companies can have mens rea

Iridium India Telecom Ltd v. Motorola Inc.

2011

The Supreme Court adopted the identification doctrine: a company can be guilty of offences requiring a guilty mind, such as cheating, through the mind of the persons who control it.

But not the other way round

Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI

2015

A company's directors cannot be made accused merely because of their position. A director is liable only for his own active role with criminal intent, or where a statute expressly makes him vicariously liable (as section 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act does).

This is the realist theory winning in practice. Modern courts treat the company as an entity that acts, intends and answers for what it does, even though they still use the language of fiction when it suits them.

Personality is the law's way of saying: this, whatever it is, can hold rights and answer for wrongs.
