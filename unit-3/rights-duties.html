Rights and duties: the grammar of law

About 24 minutes · 4 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1985, three schoolchildren in Kerala, Jehovah's Witnesses, were expelled for standing in respectful silence instead of singing the national anthem. The Supreme Court ordered them back to school. Their right to freedom of conscience was a legal right; the duty to respect the anthem under Article 51A(a) was a legal duty too. The case turned on how the two fit together.

That is what this chapter is about. Almost every legal problem you will ever meet can be restated as a question about rights and duties: who holds what, against whom, and with what remedy. Jurists from Austin to Hohfeld have tried to take that idea apart and label its pieces. Once you have their vocabulary, the rest of the law reads differently. Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986) is a good case to keep in mind as you go.

What a legal right is

Start with the word itself. In ordinary speech "right" means what is morally correct; in law it means something narrower: a claim the law will recognise and protect. A moral right ("I have a right to be thanked") becomes a legal right only when the State attaches a duty to it and stands ready to enforce that duty.

Moral (natural) right

Recognised and protected by the rules of morality or natural justice. Its violation is a moral wrong; the sanction is public opinion or conscience.

Legal right

Recognised and protected by the rules of law. Its violation is a legal wrong, and the State will provide a remedy or a punishment.

Each jurist defines a right through the lens of their own theory of law. Here are the four you must know, in the order Paranjape treats them.

A party has a right when another or others are bound or obliged by law to do or forbear towards or in regard of him.

John Austin, Lectures on Jurisprudence

Austin defines a right from the duty side. A right exists because the sovereign commands someone else to act or forbear. Criticism: it is circular (right explained by duty, duty by right) and it ignores rights against the State itself, which an Austinian sovereign could never be bound by.

A capacity residing in one man of controlling, with the assent and assistance of the State, the actions of others.

T.E. Holland, The Elements of Jurisprudence

Holland stresses control backed by the State. A legal right is the power to make others act or forbear, with the courts behind you. This puts the will of the right-holder at the centre, which is why Holland is counted among the will theorists. Criticism: infants and lunatics have rights but can control nothing.

A right is an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right. It is any interest, respect for which is a duty, and the disregard of which is a wrong.

Sir John Salmond, Jurisprudence

Salmond follows Ihering: a right is a protected interest. Not every interest is a right (my interest in my neighbour's garden looking pretty is not), only those the law chooses to protect by imposing a duty on others. This is the definition examiners expect you to build on.

A legal right is that power which a man has to make a person or persons do or refrain from doing a certain act or certain acts, so far as the power arises from society imposing a legal duty upon a person or persons.

John Chipman Gray, The Nature and Sources of the Law

Gray combines both. The interest is the end; the right is the means by which society protects it. In his words, the right is not the interest itself but the power, arising from a duty imposed by society, to protect it.

Exam lens

Do not just list four definitions. Group them: Austin defines a right through duty, Holland through will or control, Salmond through interest, and Gray through the power that protects an interest. That one sentence shows the examiner you understand why the definitions differ.

Will theory and interest theory

Behind the definitions sit two rival answers to one question: what does a right protect?

Will (choice) theory

A right protects the holder's freedom of choice. To have a right is to be in control of someone else's duty: you may demand it, waive it or enforce it. Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Windscheid, Puchta, Holland; in modern form H.L.A. Hart, who called the right-holder "a small-scale sovereign".

Interest (benefit) theory

A right protects the holder's interest or benefit. You have a right when a duty exists for your benefit, whether or not you can control it. Bentham, Ihering ("a legally protected interest"), Salmond; in modern form MacCormick and Raz.

The case for and against each

The will theory explains well why a creditor can forgive a debt, and why waiving a right is itself an exercise of it. But it struggles with rights that cannot be waived. You cannot consent to being enslaved (Article 23) or sell yourself into bonded labour, and children, persons of unsound mind, and (as you'll see in Chapter 3) idols and rivers have rights though they have no will to exercise. Their rights are exercised by guardians and next friends.

The interest theory handles those cases easily: a child has an interest that the law protects. Ihering's attack on the will theory was exactly this: the law protects the interests of those who cannot will at all. Its weakness is the opposite one. Not every beneficiary of a duty has a right. If A promises B to pay money to C, C benefits, but at common law C (a stranger to the contract) could not sue. The interest theory has to explain which beneficiaries count.

Will theory: a right is a protected choice. Interest theory: a right is a protected benefit.

Three more voices

  • Roscoe Pound built on the interest theory: law is social engineering that balances individual, public and social interests. A right is the interest the law has chosen to secure.
  • Léon Duguit, the French jurist, denied that individuals have "rights" at all. There are only social functions and the duties that go with them. Even ownership is a social function, not a personal right. Useful as a counterpoint in a long answer.
  • Hans Kelsen treated a right as the reflex of a legal duty. What matters in the pure theory is the norm imposing a sanction; a "right" is the legal power to set that sanction in motion.

Common mix-up

Salmond is an interest theorist, Holland a will theorist, even though both are analytical positivists. The two theories cut across the schools: they are about the content of a right, not the source of law.

The anatomy of a right: Salmond's five elements

Salmond says every legal right, however simple, has five elements. Learn them as a list of five nouns; then learn to spot them in any example the examiner gives.

  1. The person of inherence: the owner of the right, its subject. There can be no right without an owner, though the owner may be uncertain (a child not yet born) or a legal person.
  2. The person of incidence: the person bound by the correlative duty. It may be one person (a debtor) or the whole world (everyone must not trespass on my land).
  3. The content: the act or forbearance the person of incidence must perform. Pay the money; keep off the land.
  4. The object (subject-matter): the thing over which the right exists. A plot of land, a book's copyright, my reputation, my body.
  5. The title: the facts or events by which the right came to be vested in its owner. Purchase, gift, inheritance, contract, birth.

Owner, Bound, Act, Thing, Title is a quick way to hold the five together. Now try them on four examples.

Two refinements earn marks. First, Salmond insists that every right is a right against some person or persons: the object may be a thing, but the duty is always owed by people. Second, the title is what distinguishes "A owns this land" from "B owns this land": the content and object are the same; only the title differs.

Common mix-up

The object of a right is not the same as its content. In a sale of land, the object is the land; the content is the duty of others not to interfere with it. If you write "the content is the land", you have collapsed two elements into one.

Perfect and imperfect rights

A perfect right is one that the law recognises and enforces: it has a correlative perfect duty, and a court will grant a remedy. An imperfect right is recognised but not enforced. It is still a legal right, because it has legal effects, but there is no action to enforce it directly. Salmond gives three classic examples: claims barred by limitation, claims unenforceable for want of formality (some contracts must be in writing), and claims against foreign States or sovereigns, which are immune from suit.

Indian law gives you a neat illustration of why a time-barred debt is still a right. Section 3 of the Limitation Act, 1963 bars the remedy, not the right. So a written promise to pay a time-barred debt is a valid contract under section 25(3) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and a creditor who receives an unappropriated payment may apply it to a time-barred debt (section 60). The right was there all along; it simply lacked a remedy.

Real and personal rights (in rem and in personam)

This is the most examined pair. A real right (jus in rem) corresponds to a duty imposed on persons in general: the whole world must not interfere with my ownership of my house. A personal right (jus in personam) corresponds to a duty imposed on a determinate person: only my debtor owes me the money.

Jus in rem

Against the world at large. Usually negative (others must forbear). Usually, though not always, over a thing. Ownership, the right to reputation, the right not to be assaulted.

Jus in personam

Against a definite person. Usually positive (that person must act). Arises mostly from contract or obligations. The right to be paid, to have goods delivered, to receive services.

Note the word "usually". A real right is not necessarily a right to a thing: my right to my reputation is in rem but has no physical object. And a personal right can be negative: a restrictive covenant binds only the covenantor not to compete.

Proprietary and personal rights

Do not confuse this pair with the last one. Proprietary rights make up a person's estate (assets): land, chattels, debts, shares, patents. They have economic value and are generally transferable. Personal rights make up a person's status or personal condition: life, liberty, reputation, family relations. They have no direct money value and cannot be sold. A debt is proprietary and in personam; reputation is personal and in rem.

Rights in re propria and in re aliena

A right in re aliena (an encumbrance) is a right over property owned by someone else, which limits the owner's right. Salmond lists four kinds: leases, servitudes (easements, such as a right of way), securities (mortgages, pledges, liens) and trusts. The owner's right, burdened by the encumbrance, is the right in re propria. This pair leads straight into the next chapter: ownership is what is left when all the encumbrances have been carved out.

Hohfeld's analysis: eight fundamental conceptions

Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, an American jurist at Yale, noticed in two articles (1913 and 1917, collected as Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning) that judges used the single word "right" for four quite different things. Muddling them, he argued, led to bad reasoning. He separated them into four pairs, each with a correlative (the position of the other party) and an opposite (its negation for the same party).

Jural relationRight (claim)Privilege (liberty)PowerImmunity
CorrelativeDutyNo-rightLiabilityDisability
OppositeNo-rightDutyDisabilityLiability

Read the table like this. Correlatives always describe two people looking at the same relation from opposite ends: if X has a right against Y, Y has a duty to X. Opposites describe one person: if X has a right, X cannot at the same time have a no-right on the same point. Click any of the eight to see its pair.

The four pairs in plain words

  • Right and duty. A right in the strict sense is a claim: Y must do or not do something for X. This is the only "right" Hohfeld thought deserved the name. If you own land, you have a claim that I keep off it, and I am under a duty to keep off.
  • Privilege and no-right. A privilege (often called a liberty) is freedom from a duty: X may do something without being bound not to. You are privileged to walk on your own land; I have no-right that you stay off it. Hohfeld's famous example: if X has a privilege to enter his land, Y has no-right that X stay off.
  • Power and liability. A power is the ability to change legal relations, your own or someone else's. You can sell your land, make a will, accept an offer. Whoever's position you can alter is under a liability (a "subjection"), which need not be bad: a donee is liable to receive a gift.
  • Immunity and disability. An immunity is freedom from someone else's power: Y cannot alter X's legal position. I cannot sell your land, so you have an immunity and I a disability. Fundamental rights, read in Hohfeld's terms, are largely immunities against the State: Parliament is under a disability to make laws abridging them (Article 13(2)).

Why Hohfeld matters, and the criticism

Hohfeld's gift is precision. When a court says "the press has a right to report proceedings", is that a claim (someone must let them in), a privilege (they will not be punished for reporting), or an immunity (the legislature cannot stop them)? Each leads to a different result. Hohfeld also showed that every legal relation is between two persons; "rights over things" are really bundles of relations with every other person, which is why ownership is so often described as a bundle of rights.

The criticisms: the scheme is too abstract and says nothing about which rights the law should recognise; it treats all relations as bilateral, which fits awkwardly with duties owed to the public at large; and critics such as Dias point out that a "privilege" and an "immunity" are negative ideas, the mere absence of a duty or a liability, rather than positive conceptions. Still, Paranjape, Dias and every modern textbook treat it as the clearest analysis of rights we have.

Duties: meaning and kinds

A duty is an act that one ought to do, an act the opposite of which would be a wrong. Salmond: "a duty is an obligatory act, that is to say, it is an act the opposite of which would be a wrong." Duties, like rights, are moral or legal; a legal duty is one recognised and enforced by law, whose breach is a legal wrong.

Austin's absolute duties

Austin divided duties into relative (those with a corresponding right in someone) and absolute (those without). He gave four kinds of absolute duty:

  1. duties not regarding persons, such as duties towards God or towards animals;
  2. self-regarding duties, such as the duty not to commit suicide or not to become drunk;
  3. duties towards indeterminate persons or the community at large, such as the duty not to commit a public nuisance;
  4. duties owed to the sovereign, which can have no rights against itself under its own law.

Austin's real point was that the breach of an absolute duty is usually a crime, while the breach of a relative duty is usually a civil wrong. Criminal law protects society; civil law protects individuals.

How rights and duties relate

Is every right matched by a duty, and every duty by a right? This is the correlativity debate, and it is a favourite long question.

Correlative view

Salmond: "There can be no right without a corresponding duty, or duty without a corresponding right, any more than there can be a husband without a wife, or a father without a child." Every duty is owed to someone, even if that someone is the public or the State. Hohfeld's correlatives make the same point.

Non-correlative view

Austin: there are absolute duties with no corresponding rights. Allen and others add that criminal law imposes duties without giving anyone a personal right. Duguit goes furthest: there are only duties, as social functions.

How does Salmond answer the absolute duty argument? He agrees there are duties to which no determinate person holds the right. But he treats them as owed to the society or State, and he regards duties "towards" animals as duties owed to people (the public's interest in preventing cruelty). On the sovereign, he points out that modern constitutional States do have duties to their citizens: Indian citizens have enforceable rights against the State under Article 32, which Austin's theory cannot explain.

Paranjape's balanced conclusion: in the strict, Hohfeldian sense, a claim-right always implies a duty, because that is what a claim-right is. But not every duty creates a claim-right in a particular person, and many "rights" in the loose sense (privileges, powers, immunities) do not correspond to duties at all, but to no-rights, liabilities and disabilities.

Every claim has a duty. Not every duty has a claimant.

Rights and duties in the Indian Constitution

The Constitution is a working laboratory for every idea in this chapter.

Fundamental rights: real, personal and Hohfeldian

Part III rights are mostly rights against the State (Article 12 defines "State"). In Hohfeld's terms they are chiefly immunities: under Article 13(2) the State is under a disability to make laws that take away or abridge them, and any such law is void. Some, though, bind private persons too, making them truly rights in rem: Article 15(2) (access to shops and public places), Article 17 (abolition of untouchability), Article 23 (forced labour) and Article 24 (child labour in factories). Article 32, the right to move the Supreme Court, is itself a fundamental right: a sanctioning right that makes the others perfect.

The Directive Principles (Part IV) are the textbook example of something like imperfect rights: Article 37 says they are not enforceable by any court, yet they are "fundamental in the governance of the country" and courts use them to interpret statutes and Part III.

Fundamental duties: Article 51A

The 42nd Amendment, 1976, on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, inserted Part IVA with ten fundamental duties for every citizen. The 86th Amendment, 2002 added an eleventh, clause (k): a parent or guardian must provide opportunities for education to a child between six and fourteen. Among the others: to abide by the Constitution and respect the national flag and anthem (a), to protect the environment and have compassion for living creatures (g), to develop scientific temper and the spirit of inquiry (h), and to strive towards excellence (j).

Right and duty in tension

Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala

1986

Students who stood respectfully but did not sing the anthem had not shown disrespect, and could not be expelled. No law compelled singing; their freedom of conscience under Article 25 and expression under Article 19(1)(a) prevailed. A duty in Article 51A did not override a fundamental right in the absence of a law.

Duties as an aid to interpretation

AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS

2002

Fundamental duties are not enforceable by writ, but they are equally important as fundamental rights, and courts may use them to interpret laws and test the reasonableness of State action. The Court struck down an institutional reservation scheme that offended the duty to strive towards excellence, Article 51A(j).

Duty to the environment

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India

1987–1988 (Ganga pollution cases)

Relying on Article 51A(g), the Court directed that lessons on the protection of the environment be taught in schools and that tanneries polluting the Ganga be closed. The duty to protect the environment became a lever for enforcing the public's right to a clean environment under Article 21.

Rights for those without a will

Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja

2014

The Court banned jallikattu and bullock-cart races, reading the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 with Articles 51A(g) and (h): every citizen has a duty of compassion towards living creatures, and animals have a right to live with dignity. An interest-theory right, since bulls cannot will anything. (Covered again in Chapter 3.)

Notice how the Indian cases dissolve Austin's "absolute duties". The duty of compassion towards animals, which Austin thought had no corresponding right, is now read by the Supreme Court as protecting the animals' own interests.

Sidenote

Fundamental duties are mostly duties without a precise penalty, though several are backed by ordinary statutes: the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 for clause (a), the environment and wildlife laws for clause (g), and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 alongside clause (k).
