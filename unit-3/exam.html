Turning the concepts into marks

Unit III is the most "technical" unit of the paper: definitions, classifications, tables and cases. That is good news. Examiners reward precision here, and precision can be practised. This page gives you the questions, the shapes of the answers, and the facts to drill.

Answer skeletons

Open one, then try writing the answer from the skeleton alone. If you get stuck on a step, that's the part of the chapter to reread.

Define a legal right. Explain its essential elements.
  1. Introduction. Moral versus legal right; a legal right is recognised and protected by the State.
  2. Definitions, grouped. Austin (through duty), Holland (capacity of controlling, with the State's assistance), Salmond (interest recognised and protected by a rule of right), Gray (the power protecting an interest). One-line criticism each.
  3. Theories. Will (Holland, Hart) versus interest (Ihering, Salmond) in four lines.
  4. Salmond's five elements, each with a line: person of inherence, person of incidence, content, object, title.
  5. Worked example. A buys land from B: A, the world, forbearance, the land, the sale deed. Set it out as a table.
  6. Conclusion. Every right is a right against persons; the title is what makes it this person's right.

The worked example is what turns a list into an answer. Choose an Indian one.

Discuss the various kinds of legal rights.
  1. Introduction. Salmond's definition of a right in one line; the kinds come in pairs.
  2. Perfect / imperfect: time-barred debt; Limitation Act s. 3 bars the remedy, not the right; Contract Act s. 25(3).
  3. Positive / negative and real / personal (in rem / in personam), with a contrast table.
  4. Proprietary / personal: estate versus status; don't confuse with in rem / in personam.
  5. In re propria / in re aliena: the four encumbrances (lease, servitude, security, trust).
  6. Principal / accessory; primary / sanctioning; legal / equitable; vested / contingent; inheritable / uninheritable; public / private: one line and example each.
  7. Indian illustration. Fundamental rights as rights in rem or against the State (Arts. 12, 15(2), 17, 23); Directive Principles as near-imperfect rights (Art. 37).
Hohfeld's analysis of jural relations.
  1. The problem. Judges use "right" loosely for four different ideas (Hohfeld, 1913 and 1917).
  2. Draw the table: right, privilege, power, immunity; correlatives duty, no-right, liability, disability; opposites no-right, duty, disability, liability.
  3. Explain correlatives (two persons, one relation) and opposites (one person, negation).
  4. One example per pair: rent (claim), walking on own land (privilege), making a will or accepting an offer (power), Article 20(3) (immunity).
  5. Value. Precision; every relation is between persons; ownership as a bundle of Hohfeldian relations; fundamental rights as immunities (Art. 13(2)).
  6. Criticism. Too abstract; only bilateral relations; privilege and immunity are negative ideas (Dias).
"Rights and duties are correlative." Discuss.
  1. Define both. Salmond's right; Salmond's duty (an act the opposite of which would be a wrong).
  2. The correlative view. Salmond's "husband and wife, father and child"; Hohfeld's right–duty pair.
  3. Austin's absolute duties: towards God and animals, self-regarding, towards the public, towards the sovereign. Breach of absolute duty usually a crime.
  4. Salmond's reply: duties to the public are owed to society; modern States owe duties to citizens (Art. 32).
  5. Duguit: only duties, as social functions; Kelsen: right as reflex of duty.
  6. Indian position. Part III and Art. 51A; Bijoe Emmanuel (1986), AIIMS Students' Union (2002); A. Nagaraja (2014) turning a duty regarding animals into the animals' right.
  7. Conclusion. Every claim has a duty; not every duty has a claimant; privileges, powers and immunities pair with no-rights, liabilities and disabilities.
Define ownership. Discuss its incidents and kinds.
  1. Definitions. Austin (indefinite user, unrestricted disposition, unlimited duration), Holland (plenary control), Salmond (relation between a person and a right). Thing versus right in one line.
  2. Modern view. Bundle of rights (Dias); Honoré's eleven incidents in a line.
  3. Incidents. Possess, use, exclude, destroy, alienate, indeterminate duration, residuary character. Stress the last, with the landlord example.
  4. Kinds in a table: corporeal/incorporeal, sole/co-, co-/joint (survivorship), trust/beneficial (Indian Trusts Act s. 3), legal/equitable, vested/contingent (TPA ss. 19, 21), absolute/limited.
  5. India. Article 300A after the 44th Amendment; Vidya Devi (2020).
  6. Conclusion. Ownership is never absolute; it is the residue left after the law and other people's rights have taken their share.
What is possession? The theories of Savigny, Ihering, Salmond and Pollock.
  1. Importance. Evidence of ownership; protected in itself; mode of acquiring rights.
  2. Definition. Salmond: the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of a material object.
  3. Corpus and animus. Salmond's two relations; his four features of animus (not rightful, not ownership, not own behalf, may be general).
  4. Theories in a table: Savigny (animus domini; subjective), Ihering (outward visibility of ownership; objective; outwork of ownership), Salmond (animus possidendi), Pollock and Wright (effective physical control; possession in fact, in law, right to possess).
  5. Criticism of each in a line: Savigny's "derivative possession"; Ihering ties possession to ownership; Pollock cannot explain the servant.
  6. Conclusion. Salmond's balance: both elements, but the animus is the intent to exclude, not to own.
"Possession is nine points of the law." Discuss, with the finders cases.
  1. Meaning of the maxim. The possessor starts ahead; the challenger must prove a better title.
  2. Why. Presumption of ownership (s. 110 Evidence Act); good title against all but the true owner (Armory v. Delamirie); s. 6 Specific Relief Act; Rame Gowda (2004); adverse possession (Arts. 65 and 112, Limitation Act; s. 27).
  3. Finder versus occupier. Bridges (1851), Elwes (1886), Sharman (1896), Hannah v. Peel (1945), Parker (1982). Facts and outcome, one line each.
  4. Donaldson LJ's rules: in or attached to land, occupier; on the surface, finder unless the occupier manifested control; trespassers and employees.
  5. India. Contract Act ss. 71, 168, 169; dishonest misappropriation; Treasure Trove Act, 1878.
  6. Conclusion. An exaggeration, since the true owner always wins, but it captures how strongly the law protects the fact of possession.
What is legal personality? Discuss the theories of corporate personality.
  1. Meaning. Persona, the mask; Salmond's definition; natural and legal persons; Salmond's three classes (corporations, institutions, funds).
  2. Corporations. Aggregate and sole; Companies Act s. 9; Salomon (1897) in four lines.
  3. Theories, each with proponents and one criticism: fiction (Savigny, Salmond), concession (Dicey), realist (Gierke, Maitland), bracket (Ihering), purpose (Brinz), Kelsen.
  4. Test them on the cases. Idols fit purpose and fiction; corporate crime (Standard Chartered, 2005; Iridium, 2011) fits the realist theory.
  5. Conclusion. No one theory explains every case; Hart's advice to look at how "person" works in legal rules.

A short table of the six theories (name, jurist, idea, criticism) saves time and scores well.

Legal status of the unborn, the dead, animals and idols.
  1. Frame. Personality is a legal status, not a biological fact (Salmond).
  2. Unborn. Treated as born when for its benefit, if born alive: TPA ss. 13, 20; HSA s. 20; offences of causing miscarriage; MTP Act limits.
  3. Dead. No personality, but body, reputation and will are protected: Parmanand Katara (1995); Ashray Adhikar Abhiyan (2002); defamation of the deceased.
  4. Animals. Traditionally things; Salmond's duties owed to the public; A. Nagaraja (2014); Narayan Dutt Bhatt (2018).
  5. Idols and religious entities. Pramatha Nath Mullick (1925); Yogendra Nath Naskar (1969); SGPC v. Som Nath Dass (2000); mosque not a person (1940); Ram Lalla yes, land no (2019).
  6. Rivers. Mohd. Salim (2017), stayed by the Supreme Court.
  7. Conclusion. Indian courts use personality as a tool to protect interests through guardians.

Small things that earn marks

  • Name the jurist every time. "Salmond defines a right as…" beats "a right is defined as…".
  • Draw Hohfeld's table. It takes a minute and is the single most reliable mark-earner in this unit.
  • Use tables for every classification. Kinds of rights, kinds of ownership, kinds of possession, the theories of possession and of personality: a pair, a meaning and an example per row.
  • Give a worked example for Salmond's five elements. Asha buying land is better than an abstract list.
  • Get case facts right in one line. Who found what, where, and who won. Examiners check the outcome of the finders cases.
  • Use the Indian cases. Pramatha Nath Mullick, Som Nath Dass, Mohd. Salim and A. Nagaraja make a personality answer stand out; Article 51A and Article 300A do the same for rights and ownership.
  • Mention the stay. If you cite Mohd. Salim, add that the Supreme Court stayed it. Leaving that out is a factual error.
  • End with your own synthesis, not the last definition you wrote.

Check your edition

Definitions here follow their standard textbook wording; phrasing differs a little between editions and teachers' notes. Statutory references are to the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act as Paranjape cites them; from 1 July 2024 these were replaced by the BNS, BNSS and BSA. Citing the old section with "now the BNS" shows you are up to date.

A four-week plan for this unit

Three chapters, each dense with classifications and cases. Spread them out, and spend the extra time on the tables, which carry the marks.

Week 1Understand

Read the three chapters here alongside Paranjape. Use every widget, especially Hohfeld's square and the finders sorter. Don't memorise yet.

Week 2Write

Four timed long answers from the skeletons: elements of a right, Hohfeld, possession theories, corporate personality. Patch the gaps.

Week 3Recall

Flashcards five minutes a day. Write the kinds-of-rights and kinds-of-ownership tables from memory.

Week 4Rehearse

One timed long answer every other day. Draw Hohfeld's table and the possession theories table until they are automatic.
