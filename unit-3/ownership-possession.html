Chapter 2 of 3 · Ownership and possession

Ownership and possession: having and holding

About 26 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1980 a passenger waiting in the executive lounge at Heathrow found a gold bracelet on the floor. He handed it to British Airways staff, with his name, asking for it back if nobody claimed it. Nobody did, and the airline sold it for £850. Who was entitled to the money: the airline that occupied the lounge, or the passenger who picked the bracelet up?

The Court of Appeal said the passenger. To see why, you need the two concepts this chapter is about. Ownership is a matter of right: who the law says the thing belongs to. Possession is a matter of fact: who actually holds it, and with what intention. Most of the time they coincide. When they come apart, as with every lost bracelet, leased flat and stolen bicycle, the law needs rules for both.

What ownership is

Ownership is the fullest relation a person can have with a thing, and it is the idea on which the whole law of property rests. The definitions below differ mainly on one point: is ownership a relation to a thing, or to a right?

A right indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition, and unlimited in point of duration over a determinate thing.

John Austin, Lectures on Jurisprudence

Austin picks out three features: the owner may use the thing in any way (indefinite user), dispose of it as he likes (unrestricted disposition), and hold it without limit of time (unlimited duration). Criticism: no modern owner has unrestricted rights. Zoning rules, rent control, environmental law and the law of nuisance all limit what you may do with your own land.

Ownership is a plenary control over an object.

T.E. Holland, The Elements of Jurisprudence

Holland says the owner has three rights over the object: possession, enjoyment and disposition. "Plenary" (full) control is again an ideal rather than a description of real ownership.

Ownership, in its most comprehensive signification, denotes the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him. That which a man owns is in all cases a right.

Sir John Salmond, Jurisprudence

Salmond shifts the focus. In law, you never own a thing directly; you own rights. When we say "I own this pen", we really mean "I own the right of ownership of this pen". This is what lets Salmond fit incorporeal property (a debt, a patent, a share) into the same scheme as land and chattels. For material things he gives a narrower sense too: ownership of a material object is the right to its general use. Criticism (Dias and others): treating ownership as "owning a right" is circular, and ordinary language and law both talk about owning things.

Two later accounts are worth a line each in a long answer. Dias describes ownership as a bundle of rights (claims, liberties, powers and immunities, in Hohfeld's terms). A.M. Honoré, in his essay "Ownership" (1961), lists eleven standard incidents that together make up "full liberal ownership". Neither insists that every owner has all of them.

Exam lens

Austin and Holland describe ownership as a relation to a thing; Salmond as a relation to a right. Say that in one sentence before you quote the definitions, and the answer reads as analysis rather than a list.

The incidents of ownership

The "incidents" are the rights and features that come with ownership. Salmond and Paranjape give these:

  1. The right to possess the thing. The owner may not always have possession (a landlord does not), but he has the right to it once any lesser right ends.
  2. The right to use and enjoy it, including the right to its fruits and income.
  3. The right to exclude others from it.
  4. The right to consume, destroy or alter it.
  5. The right to alienate: to sell, gift, mortgage, lease or bequeath it.
  6. Indeterminate duration: ownership is not for a fixed time, and passes on death to heirs.
  7. Residuary character: when lesser rights (a lease, an easement, a mortgage) are carved out of ownership, the owner keeps whatever is left, and the lesser rights fall back to him when they end.

The last incident is the key to the whole idea. An owner can give away almost every one of his rights and still be the owner, because ownership is the residue that remains. A landlord who has leased his house for 99 years, mortgaged it to a bank and granted his neighbour a right of way still owns it. See for yourself.

Honoré's eleven incidents

If your teacher covers Honoré, these are his eleven: the right to possess, to use, to manage, to the income, to the capital (consume, destroy, alienate), to security (freedom from expropriation), transmissibility, absence of term, the prohibition of harmful use, liability to execution for debts, and residuary character. Notice that two of them are duties or liabilities, not rights: ownership has its burdens too.

Common mix-up

Ownership is not the same as having every incident at once. A minor owns property he cannot sell; a mortgagor owns land he cannot freely alienate; a landlord owns a house he cannot enter. The residuary character is what makes each of them the owner.

Trust and beneficial ownership

Where property is held on trust, the trustee is the owner as far as the outside world is concerned: he can sue, sell (within the trust's terms) and give a good title. But he must use the property for the beneficiary, who has the beneficial ownership. Salmond calls the trustee's ownership "nominal" and the beneficiary's "real". In India there is no separate system of equity courts. The Indian Trusts Act, 1882, section 3, defines a trust as "an obligation annexed to the ownership of property", so the trustee is the owner and the beneficiary has a right against him.

Vested and contingent ownership

Ownership is vested when the owner's title is already complete; it is contingent when the title is incomplete and will become complete only on some uncertain event. Indian law codifies the distinction in the Transfer of Property Act, 1882: section 19 (vested interest) and section 21 (contingent interest). A vested interest is not defeated by the owner's death before possession; a contingent interest may never vest at all.

Joint ownership and the coparcenary

In joint ownership, there is a right of survivorship: when one joint owner dies, his interest passes to the survivors, not to his heirs. The Mitakshara coparcenary was the classic Indian example. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 made daughters coparceners by birth (confirmed in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, 2020) and replaced survivorship with succession for interests of coparceners who die after the amendment.

Sidenote

Ownership in India is a constitutional right but no longer a fundamental one. The 44th Amendment (1978) deleted Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 and inserted Article 300A: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law." In Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh (2020) the Supreme Court described the right to property as both a constitutional right and a human right, and held that the State cannot take land without following the law.

What possession is

Possession is harder to define than ownership, and Salmond warned that it is one of the most difficult concepts in law. It matters for three reasons: it is evidence of ownership; it is protected in its own right, even against the owner; and it is the way most rights in things are acquired and transferred (by taking, by delivery).

The possession of a material object is the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of it.

Sir John Salmond, Jurisprudence

Every theory analyses possession into two elements, using the Roman terms:

corpus possessionisThe physical element: actual control over the thing, and the outward appearance of that control.
animus possidendiThe mental element: the intention to possess, to appropriate the thing's exclusive use.
possessionBoth together. Corpus without animus is mere custody; animus without corpus is a mere wish.

Salmond's corpus: two relations

For Salmond the corpus is not literally physical contact. It involves two relations. First, a relation to the thing: the possessor must be able to use it. Second, a relation to the rest of the world: there must be a reasonable expectation that others will not interfere. That expectation can come from personal presence, from secrecy (a hidden purse), from the protection of others (servants, locks, the police), or from the general respect people show for apparent possession. This is why you still possess your parked car, and the contents of your locked house while you are on holiday.

Salmond's animus: four features

  • It need not be a rightful claim. A thief possesses the stolen bicycle.
  • It need not be a claim of ownership. A tenant, a pledgee or a borrower possesses without claiming to own.
  • It need not be a claim on one's own behalf. A servant or agent may hold a thing for his master.
  • It need not be specific; it may be general. You possess the letters in your letterbox before you know they are there; a shopkeeper possesses all the goods in his shop.

Theories of possession: Savigny, Ihering, Salmond, Pollock

The jurists disagree mainly about the animus: what intention is needed, and whether it is a separate element at all. Compare them.

Savigny: the subjective theory

Friedrich Carl von Savigny, in Das Recht des Besitzes (The Law of Possession, 1803), built his theory on Roman law. Possession needs corpus, the physical power to deal with the thing and exclude others, and animus domini, the intention to hold it as owner. The emphasis on intention makes it the "subjective" theory. Criticism: it cannot explain why a tenant, a pledgee or a bailee is treated as a possessor, since none of them intends to hold as owner. Savigny had to call these "derivative possession", an exception that swallows much of the rule.

Ihering: the objective theory

Rudolf von Ihering attacked Savigny directly. For him, possession is the outward visibility of ownership: a person possesses a thing when he deals with it in the way an owner ordinarily would. The corpus is that outward relation; the animus is simply the intention implicit in such conduct, not a separate mental state to be proved. And possession is protected because it is the outwork of ownership: protecting the possessor usually protects the owner without making him prove title. The theory is "objective" because it looks at conduct. It easily covers tenants and bailees. Criticism: it makes possession too dependent on ownership, though the two can be quite separate (a thief is a possessor).

Salmond: animus possidendi

Salmond takes the middle path. The animus needed is not animus domini but animus possidendi, the intention to exclude others from the thing, which a tenant, pledgee or thief all have. His careful account of corpus (the two relations) and of animus (the four features above) is the one most Indian examiners expect.

Pollock and Wright: physical control

Sir Frederick Pollock and R.S. Wright, in An Essay on Possession in the Common Law (1888), argued from English cases rather than Roman texts. Possession in fact is effective physical control: such control as enables the holder to deal with the thing as he wishes, to the exclusion of others. They gave the animus little independent role, and distinguished three things: possession in fact, possession in law, and the right to possess. Criticism: physical control alone cannot explain why the law treats an employer, not his servant, as possessor of goods the servant holds.

How to write it up

Define possession (Salmond); explain corpus and animus. Then the theories in a table: Savigny (corpus + animus domini; subjective), Ihering (outward visibility of ownership; objective; outwork of ownership), Salmond (animus possidendi; two relations), Pollock (effective physical control; possession in fact, in law, right to possess). One criticism each. Conclude with Salmond's balance.

Mediate possession: Salmond's three cases

Salmond identifies three situations in which one person possesses through another:

  1. Through a servant or agent who holds solely for the possessor (a shop assistant holding the till).
  2. Through a borrower or bailee who holds for himself and for the possessor, but must return the thing on demand (a friend who borrowed your bicycle).
  3. Through someone who holds for himself until a term ends or a condition is met, and only then for the other (a pledgee until the debt is paid, a lessee until the lease ends).

Adverse possession in India

Adverse possession is possession nec vi, nec clam, nec precario: without force, without secrecy, without permission. It must be open, continuous and hostile to the true owner. Under the Limitation Act, 1963, a suit for possession of immovable property based on title must be filed within 12 years from when the defendant's possession becomes adverse (Article 65), or 30 years where the plaintiff is the Government (Article 112). When the period runs out, section 27 extinguishes the owner's right, and the possessor's title becomes good.

A harsh doctrine

Hemaji Waghaji Jat v. Bhikhabhai Khengarbhai Harijan

2009

The Court called the law of adverse possession "irrational, illogical and wholly disproportionate" in rewarding a wrongdoer, held the claimant had not proved the required hostile possession, and asked Parliament to reconsider the law.

A sword as well as a shield

Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur

2019

A three-judge bench held that a person who has perfected title by adverse possession can sue as plaintiff to protect it, not merely raise it as a defence. It overruled the contrary view in Gurdwara Sahib v. Gram Panchayat Village Sirthala (2014).

Acquiring possession, and "nine points of the law"

Three modes of acquisition

  • Taking (original acquisition). Possession acquired without the consent of any previous possessor. It may be rightful (catching a wild fish, which is res nullius; picking up a lost thing) or wrongful (theft).
  • Delivery (derivative acquisition). Possession transferred with the consent of the previous possessor. Delivery may be actual (handing over the thing) or constructive, of which Salmond gives three forms: traditio brevi manu (the transferee already holds the thing, say as a borrower, and now buys it), constitutum possessorium (the seller keeps the thing but now holds it as the buyer's bailee), and attornment (a warehouseman who held for the seller agrees to hold for the buyer).
  • Operation of law. Possession passes on death, on insolvency, or by order of a court.

"Possession is nine points of the law"

The proverb is an exaggeration with a real core: the possessor starts with a strong advantage, and whoever wants to dislodge him must prove a better title. In Indian law that advantage comes from several rules:

  • Possession is prima facie evidence of ownership. Under section 110 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (carried into the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023), when a person is shown to be in possession, the burden of proving that he is not the owner is on whoever says so.
  • A possessor has title against everyone except the true owner. In Armory v. Delamirie (1722), a chimney sweep's boy found a jewel and took it to a goldsmith, who kept the stones. The boy won: a finder has a good title against all but the rightful owner.
  • Possession is protected even against the owner. Under section 6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, a person dispossessed of immovable property without his consent, otherwise than in due course of law, may recover possession by a suit within six months, without having to prove title. In Rame Gowda v. M. Varadappa Naidu (2004) the Supreme Court held that even a true owner may not forcibly evict a person in settled possession; he must go to court.
  • Criminal courts protect peaceful possession to prevent breaches of the peace (section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, now carried into the BNSS, 2023).
  • Long possession becomes ownership through adverse possession and prescription.

Finders, keepers? The finders cases

When something is lost and found, three people may claim it: the true owner (who always wins if he turns up), the finder, and the occupier of the place where it was found. The cases on finder versus occupier are the best test of whether you understand possession, because they turn entirely on who had corpus and animus first.

Finder wins

Bridges v. Hawkesworth

1851

A customer found a bundle of banknotes on the floor of the public part of a shop. The shopkeeper did not know they were there. The finder won: the notes were never in the shopkeeper's custody, and the public part of a shop is open to all.

Occupier wins

Elwes v. Brigg Gas Co.

1886

A company leasing land dug foundations for a gasholder and found a prehistoric boat buried six feet down. The landowner won. The possessor of land possesses everything in or under it, even things he does not know exist.

Occupier wins

South Staffordshire Water Co. v. Sharman

1896

A worker cleaning out the company's pool found two gold rings in the mud at the bottom. The company won. Things lying in or attached to land belong to the occupier, who has the manifest intention to control it; and an employee finding in the course of employment finds for the employer.

Finder wins

Hannah v. Peel

1945

A soldier billeted in a requisitioned house found a brooch in a crevice on top of a window frame. The owner of the house, Major Peel, had never lived there. The finder won: the owner had never been in physical possession of the house, so had no possession of the brooch.

Finder wins

Parker v. British Airways Board

1982

The Heathrow bracelet. The Court of Appeal (Donaldson LJ) held for the passenger. An occupier of a building has a better claim than a finder to things on the premises only if, before the finding, he manifested an intention to exercise control over the building and the things in it. The airline had not done enough.

Occupier wins

Waverley Borough Council v. Fletcher

1996

A man using a metal detector in a public park dug up a gold brooch nine inches below the surface. The council won: an object found in land belongs to the owner or occupier, and digging was not a use of the park the public was licensed to make.

Out of these cases Donaldson LJ in Parker drew a set of rules. Learn the shape of them:

  • Things in or attached to land: the occupier wins, whether or not he knew of them (Elwes, Sharman, Waverley).
  • Things lying loose on the surface: the finder wins, unless the occupier had manifested an intention to control the place and things in it before the finding (Bridges, Parker). A bank vault: occupier. A shop floor or airport lounge: finder.
  • A finder who is a trespasser, or finds in the course of employment, does not acquire rights against the occupier or employer.
  • A finder who takes charge of the thing must take reasonable steps to find the owner.

The finder in Indian law

Indian law has no general statute on finder versus occupier; courts follow the English principles. But a finder's duties are statutory. Under section 71 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a person who finds goods belonging to another and takes them into his custody is subject to the same responsibility as a bailee. He may retain the goods until he is compensated for his trouble and expenses (section 168) and may sell them in certain cases (section 169). Keeping found property dishonestly is the offence of dishonest misappropriation (section 403 IPC, Explanation 2; now section 314 of the BNS, 2023). And the Indian Treasure Trove Act, 1878 requires a finder of treasure hidden in the soil to give notice to the Collector, who decides who is entitled to it.

Ownership and possession compared

Salmond's phrase sums it up: possession is the de facto counterpart of ownership. Ownership is the right; possession is the fact. Ihering put it the other way round: possession is the outwork, the defence line in front, of ownership.

OwnershipPossession
NatureDe jure: a matter of rightDe facto: a matter of fact (protected by law)
QuestionWhose is it?Who holds it, intending to exclude others?

And the two keep feeding into each other: possession is evidence of ownership (s. 110 IEA); long possession becomes ownership (adverse possession); and ownership usually carries the right to possess. That two-way link is the best conclusion for any long answer on this topic.

Ownership is what the law says is yours. Possession is what you hold, and the law protects that too.
