Chapter 2 of 5 · Historical school

The historical school: law grows, it is not made

About 22 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In the early 1950s, B.R. Ambedkar steered the Hindu Code Bill into Parliament: one statute to reform Hindu marriage, divorce and succession for the whole country. Its opponents had a powerful argument. Hindu law, they said, was not something a legislature could invent; it had grown over centuries out of the life and faith of the people, and differed from region to region and caste to caste. Ambedkar resigned in 1951 when the Bill stalled.

That argument was almost word for word what a German professor, Friedrich Carl von Savigny, had said in 1814 against a proposed German civil code. It is the central claim of the historical school: law is the product of a people's history and spirit, not of a legislator's will. This chapter covers Savigny, his disciple Puchta, Sir Henry Maine, and what the school still means for custom and personal law in India.

Where this fits

Positivism said law is the command of the sovereign. The historical school answered that the sovereign's commands are the least important part of law: real law comes from below, from custom and the common consciousness of the people. In Unit I you saw Savigny as one of the causes of the decline of natural law. Here you meet him as the founder of a school in his own right.

Why the school arose

The historical school was born in Germany at the start of the nineteenth century, and it was a reaction against three things at once:

  • Natural law. The eighteenth century had treated law as a set of universal truths deducible by reason, the same for every people. The historical jurists denied that any such universal law exists.
  • The French Revolution and its codes. The revolutionaries believed they could remake society by legislation. The Napoleonic Code of 1804 was imposed across much of conquered Europe, including parts of Germany.
  • Analytical positivism. Austin's view of law as a command ignored where law actually comes from. For the historical school, law is found, not made.

There were forerunners. Montesquieu, in The Spirit of Laws (1748), argued that laws must suit the climate, religion, customs and character of each nation. Edmund Burke, attacking the French Revolution in 1790, defended inherited institutions as the wisdom of generations. And the Romantic movement in Germany celebrated the folk, its language, songs and traditions, as the true expression of a nation. Savigny gave all of this a legal theory.

Savigny and the Volksgeist

Friedrich Carl von Savigny (1779–1861) was a professor of Roman law at Berlin, and later Prussian Minister for the revision of laws. He made his name with a treatise on the Roman law of possession (1803), and his great late work was the System of Modern Roman Law (1840–49).

The codification controversy, 1814

After Napoleon's defeat, A.F.J. Thibaut, a Heidelberg professor, published a pamphlet calling for a single civil code for all the German states, on the model of the French code: it would unify Germany and replace a confusing patchwork of local laws. Savigny replied the same year with On the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence (Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft). His answer was that Germany was not ready for a code. Law cannot be made to order; it must first be understood historically, and the German legal science of his day was not mature enough to state it well. A premature code would freeze the law in its present, imperfect state.

    The Volksgeist

    Savigny's positive theory is that law is an expression of the Volksgeist, the spirit or common consciousness of the people. Like language, manners and the constitution of a nation, law has a fixed character peculiar to each people, and it grows organically out of their shared life.

    Law grows with the growth, and strengthens with the strength of the people, and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality.

    Savigny, On the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence, 1814

    From this, Paranjape draws out Savigny's main propositions. They make an ideal list for a long answer:

    1. Found, not madeLaw is not the arbitrary will of a lawgiver. It develops through internal, silently operating forces, as language does.
    2. Not universalLaw is national. Each people has its own law, as it has its own language. There is no law valid for all peoples and all times.
    3. Custom firstCustom precedes legislation and is superior to it. Legislation should follow and record the people's consciousness, not override it.
    4. Simple to complexIn early societies, law lives in the consciousness of the whole people. As society matures, a class of jurists emerges to represent that consciousness and give it technical form.
    5. Jurists, then codesOnly once jurists have fully understood the historical law can it be codified, and even then the code only declares what already exists.

    Savigny described the two elements of mature law as the political element (the general principles rooted in the life of the people) and the technical element (the jurists' working-out of those principles). Lawyers, in this picture, are not makers of law but its trustees.

    Common mix-up

    Savigny did not oppose codification for ever. He opposed it for Germany in 1814, because he believed the legal science needed to draft a good code did not yet exist. Germany finally adopted a civil code, the BGB, in 1896 (in force from 1900), and the Romanist scholarship of Savigny's own school did much to shape it.

    Criticism of Savigny

    Savigny's theory is attractive and easy to attack. These are the standard criticisms; one line each in the exam is enough.

    • The Volksgeist is vague. Whose spirit? A nation contains many classes, regions, religions and castes, often with conflicting ideas of law. Savigny never says how the common consciousness is to be identified.
    • He ignored the role of legislation. Modern law is overwhelmingly statute, and statute often leads public opinion rather than following it. In India, the abolition of untouchability (Article 17), the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 and the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act 1987 were written against long-standing custom.
    • Customs can be bad. Some customs are unjust or harmful; a theory that treats custom as the purest law has no tool to reform it.
    • The Roman law paradox. Savigny was a Romanist. Roman law was received into Germany from outside; it was not the product of the German Volksgeist at all. His own life's work contradicts his theory.
    • Imitation and conquest. Many legal institutions are borrowed or imposed (the English common law in India, the French code in Europe) and still take root.
    • Conservative and pessimistic. If law can only grow slowly, conscious reform is discouraged. Critics also note that the idea of a national spirit was later misused by Nazi ideologues.
    • Over-romantic about the past, treating early law as the pure expression of a whole people when it was often the law of a dominant group.

    What he got right

    Savigny showed that law cannot be understood apart from the society that produces it, which is the bridge to the sociological school. He was right that transplanted law fails when it ignores local conditions; every legislature drafting for a diverse country still feels the force of the point. And he made custom respectable again as a source of law, after the positivists had dismissed it.

    Puchta: the disciple

    Georg Friedrich Puchta (1798–1846), Savigny's pupil, wrote a major study of customary law (Das Gewohnheitsrecht, 1828–37) and is usually credited with fixing the word Volksgeist in legal usage. He refined his teacher's theory in two ways:

    • The origin of the State. Puchta traced how the people, beginning as a natural unity, develop into a State. The State arises from the people's consciousness and is its political form.
    • General will and individual will. He saw that the will of the individual and the general will of the community can conflict, and that the State emerges partly to resolve that conflict. This gave the State, and so legislation, a larger role than Savigny allowed.

    Puchta also built an elaborate system of legal concepts arranged like a family tree, deduced one from another. Ihering later mocked this "jurisprudence of concepts" (Begriffsjurisprudenz), which is part of the story you will meet in the sociological chapter. In the exam, the safe summary is: Puchta improved on Savigny by recognising the State and the conflict between individual and general will, though he kept the Volksgeist at the root of law.

    Sir Henry Maine

    Sir Henry James Sumner Maine (1822–1888) brought the historical method to England, and he matters doubly for Indian students: from 1862 to 1869 he served as Legal Member of the Governor-General's Council in India, and much of his evidence came from Indian village communities and Hindu law. His key works are Ancient Law (1861), Village Communities in the East and West (1871) and Early History of Institutions (1875).

    Unlike Savigny, Maine was not a nationalist. He used a comparative and historical method, setting Roman, Hindu, Germanic, Irish and Slavonic law side by side to find the general pattern by which legal systems develop. He also drew the famous distinction between static and progressive societies: most societies stop developing once they have a code; a few, mainly in Western Europe, go on changing their law to meet new needs.

    From status to contract

    Maine's most famous sentence closes the fifth chapter of Ancient Law:

    The movement of the progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract.

    Sir Henry Maine, Ancient Law, 1861

    In early societies, Maine argued, the unit of law is the family, not the individual, and a person's rights and duties are fixed by birth: son, wife, slave, member of a caste or clan. That is status. Roman law shows it in the patria potestas, the father's near-absolute power over his children. As societies progress, the family dissolves as a legal unit, the individual emerges, and relations come to rest on free agreement. That is contract. Maine linked this to his patriarchal theory: the earliest society was a group of kin under the despotic authority of the eldest male.

    The word "hitherto" matters. Maine described a historical trend; he did not promise it would continue. And in the twentieth century it went into reverse. Sort these relationships: status, contract, or the modern movement back to status.

      Criticism of Maine

      • The movement has reversed. Friedmann and others point out that the welfare state fixes many rights and duties by law regardless of agreement: labour codes, minimum wages, rent control, consumer protection, standard-form contracts. A worker's terms today depend more on her legal status as an employee than on bargaining.
      • The patriarchal theory is doubtful. Anthropologists such as McLennan and Morgan argued that some early societies were matrilineal, not patriarchal.
      • The stages are not universal. Not every system passed through them in that order; Maine generalised from a small number of systems.
      • His picture of India was static. Indian law, through custom, commentary and the schools of Hindu law, changed far more than Maine allowed.

      Even so, Maine's insight survives in every Indian family-law classroom: much of personal law is still about status (who is a coparcener, a legitimate child, a wife), and the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005, which made daughters coparceners by birth, is a change in status, not contract.

      The historical school in India

      India is the best laboratory the historical school could ask for. A single Constitution governs a country whose family law is still largely personal, varying with religion, region and community.

      Custom as law

      Indian law gives custom real force. The Privy Council held in Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy (1868) that under the Hindu system of law, clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law. That is Savigny's priority of custom over text, applied by a court. The Hindu Marriage Act 1955 defines custom in section 3(a), lets customary rites validate a marriage (section 7), and preserves customary divorce (section 29(2)). Article 13(3)(a) of the Constitution expressly includes "custom or usage having in the territory of India the force of law" within "law", so a custom can be struck down if it violates fundamental rights.

      The Constitution also shields some customary law. Article 371A protects the religious and social practices and customary law of the Nagas, and Article 371G does the same for the Mizos. The Sixth Schedule gives tribal district councils powers over customary matters. In Madhu Kishwar v. State of Bihar (1996), the Supreme Court hesitated to strike down tribal customs denying women succession to land, and instead protected the women's right to livelihood: a court weighing the Volksgeist against equality.

      Personal law and the codification debate

      Savigny's argument, in India

      The Hindu Code Bill

      1947–1956

      Opponents argued that Hindu law was a living tradition that Parliament could not remake. The Bill was eventually split into four Acts: the Hindu Marriage Act 1955, the Hindu Succession Act 1956, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act 1956 and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act 1956. Thibaut, in effect, won, but only by stages.

      Personal law outside Article 13?

      State of Bombay v. Narasu Appa Mali

      Bombay High Court · 1952

      Upholding a statute banning Hindu bigamy, the court said uncodified personal laws are not "laws in force" under Article 13 and so are not tested against fundamental rights. The ruling kept personal law partly beyond constitutional review for decades.

      Custom yields to equality

      Shayara Bano v. Union of India

      2017

      A five-judge bench, by 3:2, set aside instantaneous triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat). Two of the majority judges held it manifestly arbitrary under Article 14 as a practice enforced through the Shariat Act 1937; a third held it not part of the Quranic law at all. Parliament then made it an offence in 2019.

      One code for all?

      Article 44 and the Uniform Civil Code

      Directive Principle

      The State "shall endeavour to secure" a uniform civil code. The Court urged it in Sarla Mudgal (1995). Goa has long had a common civil code, and Uttarakhand enacted one in 2024. The UCC debate is, at its root, the Thibaut–Savigny debate.

      Exam lens

      Questions often ask "Is the historical school relevant today?" or "Examine the historical school in the Indian context." The best answer is two-sided: custom and personal law show the school's insight (law reflects a people's life); social reform legislation shows its limit (law can, and sometimes must, change a people's life). Quote Collector of Madura for the first and Article 17 or the Hindu Code for the second.

      An honest assessment

      The historical school's lasting contribution is a point so obvious today that it is easy to forget it was once radical: law has a history, and that history explains it. A rule makes sense only against the society that produced it. That insight fed directly into the sociological school, which asked not where law came from but what it does.

      Its weakness is that it looks backwards. It explains why law is as it is, but gives a legislator little guidance on what law should become, and it can make bad customs look sacred. In a country committed by its Constitution to social transformation, the historical school is best used as a caution, not a master: reform must take the people with it, but reform it remains.
