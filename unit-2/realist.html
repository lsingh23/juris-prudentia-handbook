Legal realism: law is what courts do

About 22 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 2013 a two-judge bench of the Supreme Court held, in Suresh Kumar Koushal, that section 377 of the Indian Penal Code was constitutional. In 2018 a five-judge bench, in Navtej Singh Johar, held that it was not, so far as it criminalised consensual adult relations. In those five years neither section 377 nor the Constitution had changed by a single word.

So what was "the law" in 2014? A positivist points to the text. A realist points to the courts: the law is what the judges actually decide, and the best a lawyer can do is predict it. This chapter covers the American realists (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn and Frank), the Scandinavian realists (Hägerström, Olivecrona and Ross), the case against them, and the creative Indian judiciary that sometimes looks like realism in action.

Where this fits

Positivism looked at rules; the historical school looked at the past. The realists looked at behaviour: what judges, officials and lawyers actually do. Paranjape treats realism as an offshoot of the sociological approach, often described as its "left wing", because both study law in action rather than law in books. Chronologically the sociological school comes first; this unit takes realism first because it follows on so directly from the positivists it attacked.

What realism means

Legal realism grew up in the United States in the 1920s and 1930s, with a parallel movement in Sweden and Denmark. It was a revolt against formalism: the view, associated with Christopher Columbus Langdell at Harvard from the 1870s, that law is a science of principles, and that a judge reaches the right answer by logical deduction from them, like a geometer from axioms.

The realists said this was a myth. Judges make choices, and those choices are shaped by facts, policy, personality and the social world. Rules are only one input. To understand law, study what officials do. The movement drew on American pragmatism (William James, John Dewey: an idea is true if it works), on the new social sciences, and on the felt failures of formal law in an age of industrial conflict and the Great Depression.

Formalist

The law is a body of rules. The judge finds the rule, applies it to the facts, and the decision follows by logic. Certainty is the norm.

Realist

The law is what courts decide. Rules are one factor among many; the decision often comes first and the reasons later. Uncertainty is the norm.

The American realists

Oliver Wendell Holmes: the prediction theory

Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (1841–1935), Justice of the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts and then of the US Supreme Court (1902–1932), is the forerunner of the whole movement. His book The Common Law (1881) opens with one of the most quoted sentences in legal writing:

The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience.

O.W. Holmes, The Common Law, 1881

In "The Path of the Law", an 1897 lecture published in the Harvard Law Review, he gave the realists their definition:

The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law.

O.W. Holmes, The Path of the Law, 1897

To find this law, Holmes said, look at it through the eyes of the bad man: if you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict. The bad man does not care about axioms or morality; he wants to know whether he will be fined or jailed. A legal duty, on this view, is nothing but a prediction that if you do certain things you will suffer in certain ways by the judgment of a court.

Holmes the judge was famous for his dissents against formalism, above all in Lochner v. New York (1905), where the majority struck down a law limiting bakers' working hours as a violation of freedom of contract. "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics," he wrote, and "general propositions do not decide concrete cases."

John Chipman Gray: only the courts make law

John Chipman Gray (1839–1915), a Harvard professor of property law, set out in The Nature and Sources of the Law (1909) that the law of a State is composed of the rules which the courts lay down for the determination of legal rights and duties. Statutes, precedents, custom, the opinions of experts and principles of morality are only sources of law; they become law only when a court applies them. Gray liked to quote Bishop Hoadly: "Whoever hath an absolute authority to interpret any written or spoken laws, it is he who is truly the Law-giver to all intents and purposes, and not the person who first wrote or spoke them." Gray is usually counted a forerunner rather than a full realist: he still believed courts follow rules, he simply located those rules in the courts.

Karl Llewellyn: the rule-sceptic

Karl Llewellyn (1893–1962), of Columbia and later Chicago, was the movement's chief spokesman. In The Bramble Bush (1930), lectures for first-year students, he wrote: "What these officials do about disputes is, to my mind, the law itself." He later said the sentence had been taken out of context, and his mature work was far more respectful of rules.

In "Some Realism about Realism" (1931) he listed nine common points of departure of the realists. They make an excellent short note:

  1. 01Law is in flux; courts create law.
  2. 02Law is a means to social ends, not an end in itself.
  3. 03Society changes faster than law, so law constantly needs re-examination.
  4. 04A temporary divorce of "is" and "ought" for purposes of study.
  5. 05Distrust of traditional rules and concepts as descriptions of what courts actually do.
  6. 06Distrust of the theory that rules are the main factor producing decisions.
  7. 07Grouping cases into narrower, more realistic categories.
  8. 08Judging every part of law by its effects.
  9. 09A sustained, programmatic attack on legal problems along these lines.

Llewellyn's later ideas are also examinable. His law-jobs theory holds that every group must perform certain jobs to survive: disposing of trouble cases, preventive channelling of conduct, allocating authority, organising the group as a whole, and developing juristic method. He studied these jobs among the Cheyenne (The Cheyenne Way, 1941, with the anthropologist Hoebel). In The Common Law Tradition: Deciding Appeals (1960) he contrasted the grand style of judging (reasoning from principle and policy, alert to consequences) with the formal style (mechanical deduction from rules). He was also the chief architect of the American Uniform Commercial Code.

Jerome Frank: the fact-sceptic

Jerome Frank (1889–1957), a lawyer and later a judge of the US Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, was the most radical of the Americans. In Law and the Modern Mind (1930) he argued that the demand for legal certainty is a basic legal myth, a childish longing for a father-figure who has all the answers. Law is, and must be, uncertain. For any particular person, law is either actual law (a past decision on those facts) or probable law (a guess about a future decision).

Frank drew on Judge Joseph Hutcheson's essay on the judicial "hunch" (1929): judges often reach a decision by intuition and then find the rules to justify it. He mocked the conventional theory with a formula:

R × F = DThe conventional theory: Rules applied to Facts produce the Decision.
S × P = DFrank's version: the Stimuli of the case acting on the judge's Personality produce the Decision.

In Courts on Trial (1949) Frank shifted his focus to trial courts. The real uncertainty, he said, lies not in the rules but in the facts. Witnesses misremember, lie or are disbelieved; juries are swayed; trial judges' findings of fact are almost unreviewable. Even a perfectly certain rule produces uncertain results if no one can be sure what the facts will be found to be.

The phrase "what the judge had for breakfast" is often used to caricature realism. No leading realist said exactly that; the serious point is that decisions are influenced by more than rules.

    The Scandinavian realists

    The Scandinavian realists reached a similar conclusion by a very different road. They were philosophers more than lawyers, and their target was metaphysics: the belief that words like "right", "duty", "ownership" and "binding force" refer to some real but invisible thing. They wanted to explain law entirely in terms of observable facts, especially psychological facts.

    Founder · Uppsala, Sweden

    Axel Hägerström

    1868–1939 · Inquiries into the Nature of Law and Morals

    Rights and duties have no existence in the real world; they cannot be seen, touched or measured. Hägerström traced them to the magic and ritual of early Roman law, where speaking the right formula was thought to create a bond. Modern legal language, he said, still carries this belief in invisible powers.

    Sweden

    Vilhelm Lundstedt

    1882–1955 · Legal Thinking Revised

    The most extreme of the group. Talk of justice is meaningless; law should be guided instead by "social welfare", what is actually useful to society. He called his approach the method of social welfare.

    Lund, Sweden

    Karl Olivecrona

    1897–1980 · Law as Fact, 1939

    Legal rules are independent imperatives: they read like commands but are issued by no identifiable commander (contrast Austin). Their "binding force" is a feeling in the minds of the people, produced by the formal procedures of legislation and backed by an organised machinery of force. Rights are "hollow words" that work as signals guiding behaviour.

    Copenhagen, Denmark

    Alf Ross

    1899–1979 · On Law and Justice, 1958

    Valid law is the set of norms that judges actually feel bound by and apply, through their shared "normative ideology". A statement about valid law is a prediction of what courts will do, combining Holmes's behaviourism with Olivecrona's psychology. In "Tû-Tû" (1957) he compared legal concepts to a meaningless word in an imaginary tribe that nevertheless links facts to consequences.

    Common mix-up

    Olivecrona's "independent imperatives" are not Austin's commands. Austin needs a determinate sovereign who issues them; Olivecrona's whole point is that legal rules work like imperatives without anyone commanding. Write the contrast in one sentence and you've shown you understand both.

    Criticism of realism

    • It ignores the judge's own point of view. Hart's sharpest objection: a judge deciding a case does not predict what she will do. When she says "this is the law", she gives a reason for her decision, not a forecast of it. The prediction theory works for the bad man's lawyer, not for the court. Hart compared courts to a scorer in a game: the scorer's decision is final, but scoring is not "whatever the scorer says".
    • It exaggerates uncertainty. Most legal rules are never litigated. People make wills, contracts and marriages, pay taxes and obey traffic rules, and the rules work. Hard cases are a small fraction of legal life.
    • It over-concentrates on courts, especially appellate ones in Llewellyn's case, and trial courts in Frank's. Legislation, administration and private ordering get little attention.
    • It cannot explain the rules it relies on. Who counts as a "court" or a "judge" in the first place? Only a rule can say, which means rules come before decisions.
    • The Scandinavians reduce law to psychology. Feelings of being bound do not explain why a rule is valid; and Ross's "normative ideology" brings back the very norms he wanted to explain away.
    • It is better at criticism than construction. Realism told us what is wrong with formalism but offered little systematic theory of its own.

    What realism contributed

    It changed legal education: clinical legal education, fact-based teaching and the study of law with the social sciences all owe something to it. It forced honesty about the judicial role: judges do make law in hard cases, and it is better to admit it and scrutinise how. Frank's attention to fact-finding remains the most neglected and most important lesson for any trial court. And realism is the direct ancestor of the Critical Legal Studies movement, the subject of Chapter 5.

    Judicial creativity in India

    No Indian court calls itself realist. But Indian constitutional law offers a vivid illustration of the realists' central claim: the same text, read by different benches at different times, has meant very different things.

    ExploreSame words, different lawPick a pair of decisions

    Beyond overrulings, Indian courts have made law openly where the legislature was silent:

    • Basic structure. Kesavananda Bharati (1973) created a limit on constitutional amendment that appears nowhere in the text of Article 368.
    • Guidelines with the force of law. Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) laid down binding guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace, which governed until Parliament passed the POSH Act in 2013.
    • New liability rules. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987, the oleum gas leak case) replaced the English rule in Rylands v. Fletcher with absolute liability for hazardous industries, without exceptions.
    • The collegium. In the Second Judges case (S.C. Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India, 1993), the Court read "consultation" with the Chief Justice in Article 124 to mean, in effect, primacy of the judiciary in appointing judges. A realist would call that judges making the law about who the judges will be.
    • Article 142. The power to pass any order necessary for "complete justice" lets the Court fashion remedies that no statute provides.

    Frank's fact-scepticism has Indian echoes too. In Zahira Habibullah Sheikh v. State of Gujarat (2004, the Best Bakery case), the Supreme Court ordered a retrial outside the State because witnesses had turned hostile and the trial had failed to find the facts. Every Indian practitioner also knows the realist truth that the composition of a bench can matter; the Court has confirmed that the Chief Justice, as "master of the roster", allocates cases (Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India, 2018).

    Exam lens

    When asked to "critically examine" realism, use India to make both sides of the argument. For realism: Koushal and Navtej, or Gopalan and Maneka, show that law depends on judges. Against it: in each case the Court gave reasons rooted in the Constitution's text and principles, and a later bench felt bound to engage with them. Judges are not free; they are constrained, but less than formalists pretend.

    An honest assessment

    Realism is right that rules do not apply themselves, that judges choose, and that a theory of law which ignores this is incomplete. It is wrong, or at least overstated, when it suggests rules hardly matter. The truth lies in Hart's image of open texture: in the core of a rule, outcomes are predictable; in the penumbra, judges must choose, and that is where the realists' insights apply.

    For an Indian student, the realist lesson is practical. Read the judgment, not just the headnote; know which bench decided it and how large it was; and remember that a ruling can be overruled. The law of Article 21 in 1950 and in 2025 is the same text and a different law.
