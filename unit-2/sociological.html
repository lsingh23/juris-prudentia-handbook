The sociological school: law in society

About 24 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1979 a lawyer, Kapila Hingorani, read newspaper reports that thousands of undertrial prisoners in Bihar had spent longer in jail awaiting trial than the maximum sentence for their alleged offences. She filed a petition on their behalf. In Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, the Supreme Court ordered the release of thousands of them and declared the right to a speedy trial part of Article 21.

None of those prisoners had approached the Court. The old rules of standing would have shut the door. The Court opened it because the law on the books meant nothing for people who could not reach it. That is the sociological school's central idea: law must be studied, and judged, by how it actually works in society. This chapter takes you through Comte, Ihering, Ehrlich, Duguit and Roscoe Pound, and then to public interest litigation and the Directive Principles.

Where this fits

Positivism asked what law is; the historical school asked where it came from. The sociological school asks what law does, and what it ought to do for the society it serves. Realism (Chapter 3) grew out of this school. In Paranjape's phrase, the sociological approach treats law as a social phenomenon and a means of social control.

What the school claims

The sociological school emerged in the late nineteenth century, as industrialisation, urban poverty and labour conflict exposed how badly the old individualist law served modern society. Laissez-faire courts were striking down social legislation in the name of freedom of contract. A new generation of jurists said: stop studying law as a closed system of rules, and study it in its social setting.

  • Law is a social phenomenon. It is one of several means of social control, alongside religion, morality, custom and education.
  • Function over form. The question is not only "is this valid law?" but "what effect does it have, and does it serve its purpose?"
  • Law in action, not only law in books. Roscoe Pound's phrase (1910) became the school's motto.
  • Balancing interests. Law's task is to reconcile the competing claims of individuals and society.
  • Law and other sciences. Jurisprudence must use sociology, economics and psychology.

Common mix-up

Distinguish sociology of law from sociological jurisprudence. Sociology of law (Ehrlich, Max Weber) is a branch of sociology: it studies law as a social fact, descriptively. Sociological jurisprudence (Pound) is a branch of jurisprudence: it uses sociological knowledge to improve law and guide judges. Paranjape notes the distinction; a short note on it earns marks.

Comte and the forerunners

Montesquieu (The Spirit of Laws, 1748) is the distant ancestor: laws must fit the physical and social conditions of each society. But the founder of sociology itself is the French philosopher Auguste Comte (1798–1857), who coined the word "sociology" in his Course of Positive Philosophy (1830–42).

Comte held that human thought passes through three stages: the theological (events explained by gods), the metaphysical (explained by abstract forces such as "nature" or "natural rights") and the positive or scientific (explained by observed laws of phenomena). Society, too, should be studied scientifically, by observation and experiment. Comte had no use for natural rights; he spoke of individuals' duties to society, not their rights against it.

Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) applied evolutionary ideas to society and treated it as an organism; his conclusions were individualist and laissez-faire (the "Social Statics" Holmes mocked in Lochner). Comte and Spencer supplied the method; the jurists who follow applied it to law.

Ihering: law as a means to social ends

Rudolf von Ihering (1818–1892), a German jurist, is often called the father of modern sociological jurisprudence. He began as a follower of the historical school and broke with it. His two famous works are The Struggle for Law (Der Kampf ums Recht, 1872) and Law as a Means to an End (Der Zweck im Recht, 1877–83).

Law is the form of the guarantee of the conditions of life of society, assured by the State's power of constraint.

Rudolf von Ihering, Law as a Means to an End
  • Purpose is the creator of all law. Every legal rule owes its origin to a purpose, a practical motive. Law is not the silent growth of the Volksgeist (against Savigny); it is the product of conscious struggle.
  • Law as struggle. Law develops through a constant struggle between competing interests: individuals, classes, nations. Asserting one's rights is a duty owed to society.
  • Interests. The task of law is to reconcile individual interests with social interests, preventing conflict between them. This makes Ihering the founder of the "jurisprudence of interests" (Interessenjurisprudenz), developed later by Philipp Heck.
  • Levers of social motion. Society moves people by two egoistic levers, reward and coercion, and two altruistic levers, the sense of duty and love. Law uses mainly coercion.
  • Against conceptualism. Ihering satirised the "heaven of legal concepts" of Puchta's followers, where pure ideas live without contact with life.

Criticism. Ihering never gave a method for deciding which interest should win in a conflict; he placed too much weight on coercion, which fits poorly with international and constitutional law; and his "social purpose" is wide enough to justify almost anything. But his idea of law as a means of balancing interests is the root of Pound's theory.

Ehrlich: the living law

Eugen Ehrlich (1862–1922) taught at Czernowitz in Bukovina, on the edge of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, where many peoples (Romanians, Ruthenians, Germans, Jews, Armenians) lived under one official Austrian code but actually ordered their lives by their own customs. That experience shaped his Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law (1913). Its preface contains his best-known sentence:

At the present as well as at any other time, the centre of gravity of legal development lies not in legislation, nor in juristic science, nor in judicial decision, but in society itself.

Eugen Ehrlich, Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law, 1913

Ehrlich distinguished the norms for decision (the rules courts apply when disputes reach them) from the living law, the law which dominates life itself even though it has not been posited in legal propositions. Most people never go to court; their conduct is governed by the inner order of the associations they belong to: family, village, church, business, trade union. Its sources are found in modern legal documents (contracts, articles of association, wills), and in direct observation of commercial practice, customs and the life of associations.

Criticism. Ehrlich gives no test to tell legal norms from other social norms, so the living law swallows morality, etiquette and custom. He underrates legislation, which in modern societies often reshapes social life deliberately (think of India's laws against untouchability, child marriage and dowry). And he overlooks that the State is itself one of the forces shaping society. Still, every Indian lawyer who has seen how family settlements, caste panchayats or trade usages work alongside the statute book knows what he meant.

Duguit: social solidarity

Léon Duguit (1859–1928), a French professor of public law at Bordeaux, built on the sociologist Émile Durkheim's idea of the division of labour. The basic fact of social life, he said, is social solidarity, the interdependence of people. It takes two forms:

By similarityPeople share common needs and satisfy them together. Durkheim's "mechanical" solidarity.
By division of labourPeople have different needs and abilities, and exchange services. Durkheim's "organic" solidarity, growing with modern society.
The rule of lawAny rule that promotes social solidarity is law; any that hinders it is not, whoever enacts it.

From this, Duguit drew radical conclusions. He rejected the idea of State sovereignty: the State is only a group of governors, and it too is bound by the objective rule of law. He rejected subjective rights: individuals have no natural rights, only social functions and duties to perform. Even property is a social function; the owner holds it to serve social needs. And he replaced sovereignty with public service as the basis of public law, which made him a founder of modern administrative law.

Criticism. Social solidarity is a fact, not a standard; deriving what law ought to be from it repeats the is/ought confusion Duguit claimed to avoid, and Friedmann called it natural law in disguise. It is too vague to decide concrete cases. If any rule that "hinders solidarity" is not law, every individual becomes a judge of the law, a formula for anarchy. And his denial of individual rights makes it easy for an authoritarian State to claim that it alone knows what solidarity requires.

In India, though, Duguit's idea of property as a social function has a direct echo. The zamindari abolition and land reform laws, the deletion of the fundamental right to property by the 44th Amendment (1978), and the present Article 300A (property as only a constitutional right) all treat property as subject to social need.

Roscoe Pound: social engineering

Roscoe Pound (1870–1964), Dean of Harvard Law School from 1916 to 1936, is the most important name in the school and the most frequently examined. His major works include The Scope and Purpose of Sociological Jurisprudence (1911–12), An Introduction to the Philosophy of Law (1922), Social Control through Law (1942) and the five-volume Jurisprudence (1959).

Law as social engineering

Pound compared the lawyer's task to that of an engineer. Society has limited goods and endless wants; the job of law is to secure the satisfaction of as many interests as possible with the least friction and waste. Laws are tools, and legal institutions are machines to be judged by how well they work.

The engineer's method has steps: (1) make an inventory of the interests pressing for recognition; (2) select those the law should recognise; (3) fix the limits within which each is to be secured; (4) choose the means by which law will secure them; and (5) develop principles for valuing interests when they conflict.

Pound's classification of interests

Pound defined an interest as a claim, demand or desire which human beings, individually or in groups, seek to satisfy, and of which the ordering of human relations in civilised society must take account. He divided interests into three classes:

ClassWhat it includes
Individual interestsPersonality: the physical person, freedom of will, honour and reputation, privacy, belief and opinion. Domestic relations: parents and children, husband and wife. Interests of substance: property, freedom of industry and contract, promised advantages, freedom of association, continuity of employment.
Public interestsInterests of the State as a juristic person (its personality, its property), and of the State as guardian of social interests.
Social interests(1) General security (peace, health, safety, security of transactions); (2) security of social institutions (domestic, religious, political, economic); (3) general morals; (4) conservation of social resources; (5) general progress (economic, political, cultural); (6) individual life (each person's chance to live a full human life).

Pound warned that interests must be compared on the same plane: an individual interest should be weighed against another individual interest, or both should be translated into social interests first, or the social side will always win.

    The jural postulates

    To guide the valuation of interests, Pound formulated jural postulates of civilised society: the assumptions people are entitled to make in their dealings with others. In a civilised society, people must be able to assume that:

    1. 01 · AggressionOthers will commit no intentional aggression upon them. (Criminal law, tort of battery.)
    2. 02 · AcquisitionThey may control for beneficial purposes what they have discovered, created by their own labour, or acquired under the existing order. (Property.)
    3. 03 · Good faithThose they deal with will act in good faith: keep promises, meet reasonable expectations, restore what came to them by mistake. (Contract, quasi-contract.)
    4. 04 · Due careOthers will act with due care and not cast an unreasonable risk of injury upon them. (Negligence.)
    5. 05 · Dangerous thingsThose who keep things likely to escape and do harm will restrain them. (Strict liability, Rylands v. Fletcher.)

    Pound later added postulates for an industrial society: security of employment, and that society as a whole will bear the burden of misfortune that falls on individuals. These postulates are relative to time and place; Pound accepted that they change as civilisation changes.

    Criticism of Pound

    • The engineering metaphor is mechanical. Society is not a machine, and human interests cannot be measured like loads on a bridge.
    • No scale of values. Pound lists interests but gives no clear method for weighing one against another. The judge still has to choose.
    • The classification is arbitrary and overlapping. The same claim (say, freedom of speech) can be put in all three categories depending on how it is framed.
    • "Interest" is undefined in its limits. Not every desire is a legally protected interest; Pound does not explain clearly which become legal rights.
    • It assumes consensus. Critical scholars later argued that Pound's harmonising vision hides conflicts of power between classes.

    Pound's American contemporary Benjamin Cardozo, in The Nature of the Judicial Process (1921), applied a similar method from the bench: alongside logic, history and custom, the judge must use the "method of sociology", asking what the welfare of society requires.

    Balancing interests in India

    The Indian Constitution is a balancing machine in Pound's sense. Article 19(1) guarantees freedoms (individual interests), and clauses (2) to (6) let the State impose "reasonable restrictions" in the interests of public order, health, morality, security of the State and the general public (social and public interests).     PIL and the Directive Principles

    Public interest litigation

    Public interest litigation is the clearest Indian example of sociological jurisprudence: the Supreme Court reshaped procedure so that law in books could become law in action for people who could never have reached a court.

    Speedy trial

    Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar

    1979

    Petitions on behalf of undertrial prisoners led to mass releases and the recognition of speedy trial and free legal aid as part of Article 21. Often called the first PIL.

    Epistolary jurisdiction

    Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration

    1978 and 1980

    A letter from a prisoner describing torture of a fellow prisoner was treated as a writ petition. Letters and postcards became a door to the Court.

    Standing relaxed

    S.P. Gupta v. Union of India

    1981 (Judges' Transfer case)

    Bhagwati J. held that any member of the public acting bona fide can move the Court for persons who cannot approach it because of poverty, disability or social or economic disadvantage.

    Bonded labour

    Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India

    1984

    The Court appointed commissioners to investigate bonded labour in quarries and directed rehabilitation, reading Articles 21, 23 and the Directive Principles together. PIL is not adversarial but collaborative.

    The Court has also warned against misuse: PIL must not become "publicity interest" or "private interest" litigation, and courts now demand bona fides from petitioners (State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal, 2010, laid down guidelines).

    The Directive Principles

    Part IV of the Constitution reads like a list of Pound's social interests. Article 38 directs the State to secure a social order in which justice, social, economic and political, informs all institutions; Article 39(b) and (c) on the distribution of material resources and preventing concentration of wealth; Article 39A on equal justice and free legal aid; Article 41 on the right to work, education and public assistance; Article 45 on early childhood care and education; Article 47 on nutrition and public health. Article 37 makes them unenforceable in court but "fundamental in the governance of the country".

    The story of their status is itself a sociological story:

    • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Directive Principles must conform to and run subsidiary to fundamental rights.
    • Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): fundamental rights and Directive Principles are complementary; the harmony and balance between Parts III and IV is part of the basic structure.
    • Unni Krishnan v. State of A.P. (1993) read the right to education into Article 21 using Article 45; Parliament then added Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002).
    • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) read the right to livelihood into Article 21, drawing on Articles 39(a) and 41.

    The insertion of "socialist" into the Preamble (42nd Amendment, 1976) and the long series of land reform and labour laws show Indian law consciously used as an instrument of social engineering, exactly as Pound and Ihering imagined.

    Exam lens

    "Explain Pound's theory of social engineering. How far is it applicable in India?" is one of the most likely long questions in the unit. Spend half the answer on Pound (engineering, method, three classes of interests, jural postulates, criticism) and half on India: Article 19 reasonable restrictions, Directive Principles, PIL, with two named cases each.

    How to write it up

    Meaning and features; sociology of law versus sociological jurisprudence. Forerunners (Montesquieu, Comte's three stages, Spencer). Ihering (purpose, struggle, interests, four levers). Ehrlich (living law; Bukovina; norms for decision). Duguit (solidarity by similarity and by division of labour; no sovereignty, no rights, property as social function). Pound in full. India: PIL and Directive Principles. Criticism of the school, then conclusion.

    An honest assessment

    The sociological school changed the questions jurisprudence asks. After it, no serious theory could treat law as a sealed box of rules. It taught judges to look at consequences, legislators to study the effects of their laws, and lawyers to ask who the law actually reaches.

    Its weakness is that "society" and "interests" are slippery words. The school is better at describing the conflicts law must resolve than at telling us how to resolve them, and it can make the judge a social engineer with no blueprint. India's experience shows both sides: PIL brought justice to bonded labourers and undertrials, and also drew courts into policy decisions on everything from traffic to tree-felling. Use the school, but keep asking who sets the plan.
