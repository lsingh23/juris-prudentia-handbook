Exam kit · Unit II

Turning the schools into marks

10 answer skeletons · 28 flashcards · a 4-week plan

Unit II is where the paper's heaviest long questions live. Examiners want three things from a schools answer: the jurist named with his work and his key idea, a fair criticism, and a sense of how the theory plays out in Indian law. This page gives you the structure for each.

Questions that keep coming back

These are the standard forms Unit II questions take in Indian university papers. If you can write only five full practice answers, write Austin's command theory, Kelsen's pure theory, Savigny's Volksgeist, Pound's social engineering and the realist school.

Answer skeletons

Open one, then try writing the answer from the skeleton alone. If you get stuck on a step, that's the part of the chapter to reread.

Austin's command theory: explain, criticise, apply to India.
  1. Introduction. Analytical positivism; separation of law and morals; Bentham as founder, Austin as systematiser (Province of Jurisprudence Determined, 1832).
  2. Definition. "A rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him." Positive law as the command of the sovereign.
  3. Three elements. Command, duty, sanction, one line each.
  4. Sovereign. Quote the definition; marks: determinate, habitual obedience of the bulk, not habitually obedient, indivisible, illimitable.
  5. Laws properly and improperly so called (table or tree): divine, positive, positive morality, metaphor. Exceptions: declaratory, repealing, imperfect obligation.
  6. Criticism. Maine and Ranjit Singh; permissive laws (Hart); judge-made law; sanction not the only motive; divided sovereignty; international law; Hart's gunman and Rex I/Rex II.
  7. India. No illimitable sovereign: Article 13, Articles 245–246, judicial review, basic structure (Kesavananda, 1973), "We, the People".
  8. Conclusion. Clarity and method are lasting merits; incomplete as a theory of law.

Learn the sovereignty definition verbatim. It is worth marks by itself.

Hart's concept of law.
  1. Context. The Concept of Law (1961): positivism rebuilt after rejecting Austin.
  2. Critique of Austin. Gunman situation writ large; obliged versus obligation; variety of laws; persistence and continuity.
  3. Primary and secondary rules. The three defects (uncertainty, static quality, inefficiency) and their remedies (recognition, change, adjudication) in a table.
  4. Rule of recognition. Master rule; exists as social fact; internal point of view; two minimum conditions for a legal system.
  5. Open texture. Core and penumbra; "no vehicles in the park"; judicial discretion.
  6. Minimum content of natural law. Five truisms.
  7. Debates. Hart–Fuller (1958, grudge informer); Dworkin's principles; soft positivism in the Postscript.
  8. Conclusion. The most influential modern positivist account.
Kelsen's pure theory and the Grundnorm of the Indian Constitution.
  1. Purity. Free from ethics, politics, sociology, psychology, history. Justice is irrational ideal.
  2. Norms. "Ought" propositions; if A, then sanction B ought to follow; addressed to officials.
  3. Validity. Each norm valid because of a higher norm.
  4. Grundnorm. Presupposed, not posited; must be efficacious "by and large".
  5. Hierarchy (draw it): Grundnorm → Constitution → statutes → delegated legislation → individual norms. Concretisation.
  6. India. Constitution at the top of posited norms; basic structure as a higher-order test (Kesavananda 1973; Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain 1975; Minerva Mills 1980); ultra vires.
  7. Criticism. Vague Grundnorm; efficacy makes it impure; sanction-centred; ignores values; primacy of international law.
  8. Conclusion. A rigorous model of legal structure, silent on content.
Revolutions and the validity of a new legal order.
  1. Kelsen's position. A successful revolution creates a new Grundnorm; efficacy is the test.
  2. State v. Dosso (Pakistan, 1958): Munir C.J. applied Kelsen; martial law order the new legal order.
  3. Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex p. Matovu (1966): Obote's 1966 Constitution valid as a successful revolution; relied on Dosso.
  4. Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke (PC, [1969] 1 AC 645): Rhodesian UDI not a successful revolution while Britain maintained its claim; regulations void. Lord Pearce dissented on necessity.
  5. Asma Jilani v. Government of Punjab (1972): Dosso overruled; Kelsen misapplied; Yahya Khan a usurper.
  6. Evaluation. Kelsen describes after the event; courts should not legitimise a usurpation in progress. Doctrine of necessity (Nusrat Bhutto, 1977) as a later twist.
Savigny's Volksgeist and its relevance in India.
  1. Background. Reaction against natural law, the French Revolution and codification; Montesquieu and Burke as forerunners.
  2. The 1814 controversy. Thibaut's call for a code; Savigny's Vocation of Our Age: Germany not ready.
  3. Volksgeist. Law like language; "grows with the growth…" quote.
  4. Propositions. Found not made; national not universal; custom above legislation; simple to complex; jurists, then codes.
  5. Criticism. Vague; ignores legislation; bad customs; Roman law paradox; borrowing and conquest; conservative.
  6. Puchta in two lines.
  7. India. Collector of Madura (1868); HMA ss. 3(a), 7, 29(2); Article 13(3)(a); Articles 371A, 371G; Hindu Code Bill; Shayara Bano (2017); Article 44.
  8. Conclusion. Valuable caution; poor guide to reform.
Sir Henry Maine and "status to contract".
  1. Maine. Ancient Law (1861); Legal Member in India 1862–69; comparative and historical method.
  2. Static and progressive societies.
  3. Stages. Themistes → customary law (aristocracy) → codes → fictions, equity, legislation.
  4. Status to contract. Quote; family as the unit; patria potestas; the individual emerges; patriarchal theory.
  5. Reversal. Welfare state, labour codes, rent control, consumer law, standard forms (Friedmann).
  6. Criticism. Patriarchal theory doubted; stages not universal; static picture of India.
  7. Savigny versus Maine table.
The American realist movement.
  1. Meaning and background. Revolt against Langdell's formalism; pragmatism; law in action.
  2. Holmes. "Life of the law…experience"; prediction theory; the bad man; Lochner dissent.
  3. Gray. Law is the rules courts lay down; statutes are sources; Bishop Hoadly.
  4. Llewellyn. "What officials do about disputes"; the nine points; law-jobs; grand and formal style.
  5. Frank. Basic legal myth; actual and probable law; hunch; S × P = D; Courts on Trial.
  6. Rule-sceptics versus fact-sceptics table.
  7. Scandinavians in a paragraph; American/Scandinavian contrast.
  8. Criticism. Hart's internal point of view; exaggerated uncertainty; court-centred.
  9. India. Gopalan to Maneka; Koushal to Navtej; Vishaka; M.C. Mehta absolute liability.
Pound's social engineering and its application in India.
  1. Sociological school in brief. Law in society; law in books versus law in action.
  2. Social engineering. Satisfy the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction and waste.
  3. Method. Inventory, selection, limits, means, valuation.
  4. Interests (table): individual (personality, domestic, substance), public, social (six).
  5. Jural postulates. Five, one line each, with the field of law each supports.
  6. Criticism. Mechanical metaphor; no scale of values; overlapping classes.
  7. India. Article 19 reasonable restrictions; Directive Principles (Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 45, 47); Minerva Mills harmony; PIL (Hussainara, S.P. Gupta, Bandhua Mukti Morcha); Olga Tellis.
  8. Conclusion. Indian constitutional law is social engineering in practice.

Write the six social interests as a numbered list. Examiners look for them.

Ihering, Ehrlich and Duguit: a comparative note.
  1. Ihering. Law as a Means to an End; purpose creates law; struggle; individual and social interests; four levers.
  2. Ehrlich. Centre of gravity in society; living law versus norms for decision; Bukovina; criticism: no test for legal norms.
  3. Duguit. Social solidarity (similarity, division of labour); no sovereignty, no subjective rights; property as social function; public service; criticism: is/ought, vagueness.
  4. Common thread. Law serves social ends; the State is not the only source of law.
Critically examine the Critical Legal Studies movement.
  1. Origins. Conference on CLS, Wisconsin–Madison, 1977; roots in realism, Marxism, Gramsci, Derrida and Foucault.
  2. Unger. Formalism and objectivism; deviationist doctrine; empowered democracy.
  3. Kennedy. Individualism and altruism; rules and standards; fundamental contradiction; legal education and hierarchy.
  4. Core ideas. Indeterminacy; law is politics; legitimation and hegemony; critique of public/private.
  5. Methods. Trashing (Kelman); deconstruction.
  6. Offshoots. Feminist jurisprudence (MacKinnon, Gilligan; Mathura, Vishaka, Joseph Shine); critical race theory (Bell, Crenshaw; caste and Patan Jamal Vali).
  7. Criticism. Nihilism (Carrington, Fiss); exaggerated indeterminacy; self-refuting; no programme; rights critique.
  8. Conclusion. A critic of complacency, not a replacement for law.

Small things that earn marks

  • Name the jurist, the work and the year. "Austin, in The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832)…" signals command of the subject in one line.
  • Quote one sentence per jurist. Holmes's prediction, Savigny's "grows with the growth", Maine's "status to contract", Ehrlich's "centre of gravity". Set it on its own line.
  • Pair every theory with a criticism, and every criticism with the name of the critic: Hart on Austin, Hart on the realists, Friedmann on Maine and Duguit.
  • Draw something. Austin's tree of laws, Kelsen's hierarchy, Hart's defects-and-remedies table, Pound's interests table, the rule/fact-sceptic table.
  • Bring every school to India. Positivism: Kesavananda. Historical: Collector of Madura, the Hindu Code. Realist: Koushal and Navtej. Sociological: PIL and Part IV. CLS: Mathura and Joseph Shine.
  • Compare schools. A short closing paragraph contrasting the school with its rival (Austin versus Savigny, Pound versus Ehrlich) shows judgment.
  • Get the case names right. Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex parte Matovu; Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke. Examiners notice.

Check your edition

Quotations here follow their standard textbook wording; translations of Savigny, Ihering and Ehrlich vary between editions. If your teacher dictated a particular version, use theirs in the exam; the meaning is what matters.

A four-week plan for this unit

Unit II has five schools and a lot of names. The trick is to learn each school as a story (what it rejected, what it claimed, who criticised it, where India fits) rather than a list.

Week 1Understand

Read the five chapters here, alongside Paranjape. Use every widget. Build a one-page timeline of all the jurists.

Week 2Write

Five timed long answers from the skeletons: Austin, Kelsen, Savigny, realism, Pound. Compare with the chapters and patch the gaps.

Week 3Recall

Flashcards for five minutes a day. Write six short notes from memory: Hart–Fuller, Dosso, Puchta, Scandinavian realism, Duguit, CLS.

Week 4Rehearse

One timed long answer every other day. Draw Kelsen's hierarchy, Austin's tree and Pound's interests table from memory until they're automatic.
