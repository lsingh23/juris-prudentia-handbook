Critical legal studies: law is politics

About 22 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1979 the Supreme Court acquitted two policemen of raping Mathura, a young tribal girl, in a police station. Because she had not raised an alarm and bore no injuries, the Court reasoned, she must have consented. Four law professors wrote an open letter to the Chief Justice tearing the judgment apart. The protest that followed changed the law of rape in 1983.

The judgment applied the ordinary rules of evidence and consent, neutrally and logically. Its critics asked a different question: whose experience did those neutral rules reflect? That question, that law's claim to neutrality hides choices which favour some groups over others, is the heart of Critical Legal Studies (CLS) and the movements it inspired. This chapter covers the origins of CLS, its leading thinkers, its main ideas, its offshoots in feminist jurisprudence and critical race theory, and the case against it.

Where this fits

The realists showed that judges choose. CLS took the next step: if judges choose, their choices are political, and legal doctrine is a way of dressing politics up as logic. Of all the schools in this unit, CLS is the most recent and the most radical. It is less a single theory than a movement of critics.

Origins: the 1977 conference

CLS took organised shape at the first Conference on Critical Legal Studies, held at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in May 1977. Its organisers included Duncan Kennedy, David Trubek, Mark Tushnet and Morton Horwitz; Roberto Unger soon became its best-known theorist. Most were young American law teachers shaped by the civil rights movement, opposition to the Vietnam War and the student protests of the 1960s. The movement peaked in the 1980s, especially at Harvard Law School, where battles over hiring and tenure became known as a faculty "civil war".

CLS drew on several roots:

  • American legal realism. The realists' rule-scepticism is the movement's direct ancestor. CLS has been called "realism with a political edge".
  • Marxism and the Frankfurt School. Law as part of the superstructure that serves dominant classes; Antonio Gramsci's idea of hegemony, domination maintained by consent rather than force.
  • Structuralism and post-structuralism. Jacques Derrida's deconstruction and Michel Foucault's analysis of power and knowledge.

The movement spread to Britain with the Critical Legal Conference (from 1984), and its methods passed to feminist legal theory and critical race theory, which you'll meet below.

Roberto Unger and Duncan Kennedy

Roberto Mangabeira Unger

Roberto Mangabeira Unger (born 1947), a Brazilian philosopher who joined the Harvard faculty in the early 1970s, wrote Knowledge and Politics (1975), Law in Modern Society (1976), The Critical Legal Studies Movement (1983 in the Harvard Law Review; book 1986) and False Necessity (1987). He later served as a minister in Brazil's government.

Unger identified two targets of the critique:

Formalism

The belief that legal reasoning is a method distinct from open-ended political and ideological dispute: that judges can derive answers from law rather than choosing them.

Objectivism

The belief that the body of legal materials (statutes, cases, accepted ideals) embodies a coherent and defensible scheme of human association, rather than a patchwork of compromises won by contending interests.

But Unger was not only negative. He proposed deviationist doctrine: using the conflicting principles inside law (the "counter-principles" that sit beside dominant rules) to push doctrine toward a more democratic order. His political programme of empowered democracy included "destabilisation rights", rights to break open entrenched institutions and hierarchies. He called his vision "superliberalism": liberalism's ideals carried further than liberal institutions allow.

Duncan Kennedy

Duncan Kennedy (born 1942), Professor at Harvard, is the movement's leading organiser and most cited writer.

  • "Form and Substance in Private Law Adjudication" (1976). Private law is torn between two forms, clear rules and flexible standards, and two substantive visions, individualism (self-reliance; people must look after themselves) and altruism (people must share and sacrifice for others). Neither ever wins; judges switch between them.
  • The "fundamental contradiction" (1979). In "The Structure of Blackstone's Commentaries" Kennedy wrote that relations with others are both necessary to our freedom and a threat to it. Every legal doctrine is an attempt to manage this contradiction, and none can resolve it. (He later disowned the phrase, but it remains famous.)
  • "Legal Education and the Reproduction of Hierarchy" (1982). Law schools train students to accept the hierarchies of the profession and society as natural and inevitable, through the case method, the Socratic classroom and the ladder of firms.

Other names worth a line: Morton Horwitz (The Transformation of American Law, 1780–1860, 1977) showed nineteenth-century judges remaking the common law to favour commercial and industrial interests; Mark Tushnet ("An Essay on Rights", 1984) argued that rights are unstable and indeterminate; Alan Freeman (1978) argued that anti-discrimination law adopts the "perpetrator perspective" and so legitimises continuing racial inequality; Peter Gabel wrote on the alienating effect of rights consciousness.

The core ideas

Law is not neutral, not determinate and not separate from politics.

1. Indeterminacy

Legal materials do not determine outcomes. For almost every rule there is a counter-rule, for every principle a counter-principle, and for every precedent a way to distinguish it. A skilled lawyer can argue either side, and a judge can justify either result. The realists said the same about rules; CLS said it about the whole structure of legal reasoning. Try it.

2. Law is politics

If doctrine does not determine outcomes, something else does: the values, interests and ideology of judges and of the legal culture. "Law is politics" is CLS's slogan. It does not mean judges are corrupt or partisan in the electoral sense; it means that every legal choice distributes power and resources, and that pretending otherwise conceals the choice.

3. Legitimation and hegemony

Law's most important work, for CLS, is ideological. By presenting existing hierarchies (employer and worker, landlord and tenant, men and women) as the natural result of neutral rules and free choices, law makes them seem legitimate and inevitable. People accept their position because the law tells them it is fair. This is Gramsci's hegemony applied to law. The related idea of reification describes how human-made arrangements, like "the market" or "the corporation", come to seem like facts of nature.

4. Critique of liberal dichotomies

Liberal law depends on oppositions: public and private, State and market, contract and tort, rights and policy, law and politics. CLS argues these lines are arbitrary and that each side depends on the other. The "private" family and the "free" market, for example, are created and shaped by State law at every point.

Trashing and deconstruction

CLS developed two characteristic methods.

  • Trashing. Mark Kelman's term ("Trashing", Stanford Law Review, 1984): take specific arguments very seriously in their own terms; discover they are actually foolish; and then look for some (external observer's) order in the internally contradictory, incoherent chaos we've exposed. In practice: show that a doctrine contradicts itself, that it cannot produce the outcomes it claims to, and that its real effect serves particular interests.
  • Deconstruction. Borrowed from Derrida. Every legal text rests on binary oppositions in which one term is privileged (reason over emotion, public over private, rule over exception). Deconstruction shows that the privileged term depends on the one it suppresses, and that the hierarchy could be reversed.

Offshoot: feminist jurisprudence

Feminist legal theory shares CLS's suspicion of neutrality, but asks a sharper question: neutral for whom? Its answer is that law has been made by men, from men's experience, and that its "objective" standards are often male standards. There are several strands:

StrandCore claimAssociated with
Liberal feminismWomen and men are equal; law should treat them alike and remove formal barriersEqual-treatment litigation, e.g. Ruth Bader Ginsburg's cases in the 1970s
Radical (dominance) feminismThe problem is not difference but male dominance; law institutionalises it, especially in sexualityCatharine MacKinnon, Toward a Feminist Theory of the State (1989): "the state is male in the feminist sense"
Cultural (relational) feminismWomen's moral reasoning stresses care and relationships; law values only abstract rights and rulesCarol Gilligan, In a Different Voice (1982): the "ethic of care"
Postmodern feminismThere is no single "woman's experience"; categories of gender are themselves constructedInfluenced by Foucault and Derrida; intersectional critics

A key feminist target is the public/private divide (Frances Olsen, "The Family and the Market", 1983): by treating the home as private, law left domestic violence and marital inequality outside its reach.

Feminist jurisprudence in Indian courts

The critique

Tukaram v. State of Maharashtra (Mathura)

1979

Acquittal on the reasoning that absence of resistance meant consent. The open letter by Upendra Baxi, Lotika Sarkar, Raghunath Kelkar and Vasudha Dhagamwar led to the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 1983, which introduced custodial rape provisions and a presumption of absence of consent in certain cases (section 114A, Evidence Act).

Filling the gap

Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan

1997

Drawing on CEDAW, the Court laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace, treating it as a violation of Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21.

Against "romantic paternalism"

Anuj Garg v. Hotel Association of India

2007

The Court struck down a ban on women working in bars, holding that "protective" laws based on stereotypes of women's roles violate equality.

Wife as property

Joseph Shine v. Union of India

2018

Section 497 IPC (adultery) was struck down: it treated the wife as the husband's property, since the offence disappeared if the husband consented. A textbook feminist critique turned into constitutional law.

Offshoot: critical race theory

Critical race theory (CRT) grew in the late 1970s and 1980s among scholars of colour who shared CLS's critique of neutrality but felt CLS ignored race. Its key figures:

  • Derrick Bell: the interest convergence thesis (1980). Gains for Black Americans, such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), came only when they converged with the interests of white elites (for example, America's Cold War image abroad).
  • Kimberlé Crenshaw: intersectionality (1989). A Black woman may suffer discrimination that is neither simply racial nor simply sexual, and that anti-discrimination law, by examining one ground at a time, cannot see.
  • Richard Delgado and Mari Matsuda: storytelling and "looking to the bottom", taking the experience of the oppressed as a source of legal knowledge.
  • Patricia Williams (The Alchemy of Race and Rights, 1991): against CLS's rights critique, she argued that for those who have never had rights, rights are precious, not illusions.

India's closest parallel is caste. Article 17 abolishes untouchability, and Articles 15(4) and 16(4) permit special provisions, reflecting the recognition that formal equality alone entrenches historical hierarchy. The Supreme Court used intersectionality by name in Patan Jamal Vali v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2021), considering how caste, gender and disability combined in a crime against a blind Dalit woman.

    Criticism of CLS

    • Nihilism. If law is only politics, why respect it? Paul Carrington ("Of Law and the River", 1984) caused uproar by suggesting that nihilist teachers had an ethical duty to leave the law school. Owen Fiss ("The Death of the Law?", 1986) warned that CLS threatens the very idea of law as a public, reasoned enterprise.
    • Indeterminacy is exaggerated. Most legal questions have clear answers; that is why most disputes never reach court. Hart's core of settled meaning answers CLS as much as it answered the realists.
    • Self-defeating. If all legal argument is politics, so is CLS's own argument. Why should anyone accept it as true rather than as one more political position?
    • No positive programme. CLS is far better at tearing down than building. Unger's empowered democracy is the main exception, and it is vague.
    • Elitist and obscure. A movement claiming to speak for the dispossessed was based in elite law schools and wrote in dense theoretical jargon.
    • The rights critique hurt the weak. Critical race and feminist scholars argued that dismissing rights as illusions is a luxury for those who already have them.

    What CLS contributed

    CLS made it impossible to treat legal doctrine as innocent. It showed how legal reasoning can disguise choices, how education shapes lawyers, and how the "natural" categories of law were built. Its offshoots, feminist jurisprudence and critical race theory, have had a far larger practical influence than CLS itself, in India as elsewhere. In India, kindred critical work, such as Upendra Baxi's writing on the crisis of the Indian legal system and Marc Galanter's studies of why the "haves" come out ahead in litigation, asks the same question CLS asked: whom does the law actually serve?

    Exam lens

    CLS questions usually come as a short note or "Critically examine the CLS movement." Hit five points: origins (1977, Wisconsin, realism plus the New Left); Unger (formalism and objectivism); Kennedy (individualism and altruism; legal education); the core ideas (indeterminacy, law is politics, legitimation); and criticism (nihilism, exaggeration). Add one Indian example: Mathura and the 1983 amendment, or Joseph Shine.

    How to write it up

    Introduction: CLS as a movement, not a single theory. Origins and roots. Unger and Kennedy. Core ideas under four headings. Methods: trashing and deconstruction. Offshoots: feminist jurisprudence (strands, Indian cases) and critical race theory (Bell, Crenshaw; caste in India). Criticism. Conclusion: a necessary critic of complacency, not a replacement for law.

    An honest assessment

    CLS is easiest to understand as the last step in a long argument. Positivism separated law from morals; realism showed that judges still choose; CLS concluded that those choices are political, and that law's appearance of neutrality is part of how power works. Each step was partly right.

    But a working legal system cannot run on critique alone. The Mathura story shows both halves: critics exposed the bias inside "neutral" law, and then they used law (an amendment, a presumption, a statute) to fix it. The lasting lesson of CLS for an Indian lawyer is to keep asking whose interests a rule serves, while still believing that rules, rights and reasoned judgments are worth fighting for.
