Chapter 1 of 5 · Positivism

Legal positivism: law as it is

About 26 minutes · 3 interactive widgets · 5-question check

In 1958 a general seized power in Pakistan, tore up the Constitution and declared martial law. Within weeks the Supreme Court was asked whether the old Constitution still governed. Chief Justice Muhammad Munir opened a book by an Austrian professor, Hans Kelsen, and concluded that a successful revolution is itself a law-creating fact. The old Constitution was gone; the general's orders were law.

That is legal positivism at its sharpest. Positivists insist that whether something is law is one question, and whether it is good law is another. This chapter takes you through the four names you must know, Bentham, Austin, Hart and Kelsen, and then shows what happens when their theories meet coups, constitutions and the Indian Supreme Court.

Where this fits

In Unit I you met natural law, which judges law against a higher standard of reason and justice. Positivism is its great rival. Unit II is a tour of the schools of legal theory, and each one answers the question "what is law?" differently. Positivism answers: law is what has been laid down (positum) by a recognised authority. Paranjape calls it the analytical or imperative school.

What positivism claims

"Positive" here has nothing to do with being optimistic. It comes from the Latin positum, "laid down" or "posited". Positive law is law that has been laid down by human beings, as opposed to law said to exist in nature or reason. From that starting point, positivists share a family of claims:

  • The separation thesis. Law as it is and law as it ought to be are distinct. A rule can be valid law and still be morally wicked. This is the core of the school.
  • The sources thesis. Whether a rule is law depends on its source (who made it, and how), not on its merits.
  • Analysis of concepts. The jurist's job is to analyse legal concepts as they are found in developed legal systems: rights, duties, sovereignty, sanction, person, possession.
  • State-centred law. Law is usually traced to the State: to a sovereign (Austin), a rule of recognition (Hart) or a basic norm (Kelsen).

The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another.

That line is Austin's, and almost every positivist would sign it. Notice what it does not say. It does not say law should never be criticised. Bentham, the founder, was the fiercest legal reformer of his age. It says only that criticism is a separate job from description.

Natural law asks

Is this rule consistent with reason and justice? An unjust law lacks the full claim to be law (lex iniusta non est lex).

Positivism asks

Was this rule made by the recognised authority in the recognised way? If yes, it is law. Whether it should be obeyed or reformed is a moral question for later.

Common mix-up

Do not confuse legal positivism with Auguste Comte's philosophical "positivism" (the idea that only scientific, observable knowledge counts), which you'll meet in the sociological chapter. They share a word and a dislike of metaphysics, but legal positivism is about the source of law, not about scientific method.

Bentham: the founder

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) is the real father of English positivism, though for a century he was overshadowed by his disciple Austin. His great jurisprudential work, Of Laws in General, was written around 1782 but lay in manuscript until 1945, and appeared in a full edition edited by H.L.A. Hart only in 1970. That is why Austin's name, not Bentham's, became attached to the command theory.

Bentham defined a law as an assemblage of signs declarative of a volition conceived or adopted by the sovereign in a state, concerning the conduct to be observed in a certain case by a certain person or class of persons. Law, in other words, is the expressed will of the sovereign.

What Bentham added

  • Expository and censorial jurisprudence. The expositor tells us what the law is; the censor tells us what it ought to be. You met this in the Nature chapter. It is the separation thesis in its first form.
  • Utility. The test for what law ought to be is the principle of utility: the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Nature has placed mankind under two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure (An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789).
  • Natural rights rejected. "Natural rights is simple nonsense; natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts."
  • Four sanctions. Physical, political (legal), moral and religious. Only the political sanction is the law's own.
  • Eight aspects of a law. Source, subjects, objects, extent, aspects, force, remedial appendages and expression: a remarkably modern scheme for analysing any statute.
  • Codification. Bentham coined the word "codification" and wanted every country to have a complete, rational code. His influence reached India through Macaulay and the Indian Penal Code of 1860.

Bentham's theory was in some ways subtler than Austin's. He accepted that a sovereign's power could be limited by "express convention", and he recognised laws backed by rewards (praemial laws), not only punishments.

Austin's command theory

John Austin (1790–1859) was the first Professor of Jurisprudence at the new University of London. His lectures were published as The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832), and his fuller Lectures on Jurisprudence were published by his widow, Sarah Austin, in 1863. His aim was to mark off the "province" of jurisprudence, to say exactly which rules count as law.

A law, in the most general and comprehensive acceptation in which the term is employed, is a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him.

John Austin, The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, 1832

Positive law, the proper subject of jurisprudence, is a command of the sovereign, backed by a sanction. Each word does work.

CommandAn expression of a wish that another act or forbear, by someone with the power to inflict an evil if the wish is disregarded.
DutyThe person commanded is bound, or obliged, by the command. Command and duty are correlative.
SanctionThe evil likely to follow disobedience. Without a sanction, there is no command, and so no law.

The sovereign

Whose commands count? Austin's definition of the sovereign is a guaranteed short question. Learn it word for word:

If a determinate human superior, not in a habit of obedience to a like superior, receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, that determinate superior is sovereign in that society, and the society (including the superior) is a society political and independent.

John Austin, Lecture VI

Pull out the elements and you have the marks of Austinian sovereignty:

  • DeterminateA specific person or body of persons, not "the people" in general or public opinion.
  • Habitual obedienceFrom the bulk of society, as a habit; not occasional or unanimous obedience.
  • Not obedientThe sovereign is not in a habit of obedience to any other human superior.
  • IndivisibleSovereignty cannot be split between two bodies; in a federation Austin had to locate it in the body that can amend the constitution.
  • IllimitableThe sovereign cannot be legally limited. Constitutional limits on it are only positive morality.

Laws properly and improperly so called

Austin then drew his fence. Not everything we call "law" is law in the strict sense:

Austin's categoryWhat it coversInside jurisprudence?
Divine lawLaws set by God to men. Laws properly so called, but not positive.No
Positive lawLaws set by political superiors (the sovereign, or those to whom it delegates) to political inferiors.Yes: the only subject
Positive moralityRules set by men not as political superiors (a club's rules, a father to his children), and laws "by analogy": honour, fashion, international law, constitutional law in part.No
Laws by metaphorThe "laws" of science: gravity, motion, supply and demand. They describe; they do not command.No

Austin admitted three kinds of law that are not really commands, yet belong to the province of jurisprudence: declaratory or explanatory laws, repealing laws, and laws of imperfect obligation (those without a sanction). And custom, for Austin, is not law until the courts adopt it; then it becomes a tacit command of the sovereign, who permits the judges to enforce it.

    Criticism of Austin

    The criticisms of Austin are among the most frequently examined paragraphs in the whole paper. Group them under his three pillars and add the Indian position at the end.

    Against "command"

    • Customary law. Sir Henry Maine pointed to Maharaja Ranjit Singh of Punjab, an absolute despot who never once issued a "command" in Austin's sense; the lives of his subjects were governed by immemorial custom. Much of the world's law has never been anyone's command.
    • Permissive and power-conferring laws. A law that lets you make a will, marry or form a company commands nothing. Hart's critique made this central.
    • Judge-made law. Calling precedent a "tacit command" of a sovereign who never saw the case is a fiction.
    • Law is also binding on the lawmaker. A modern statute binds the legislators who passed it. A command is addressed to others.

    Against "sovereign"

    • Sovereignty is often divided and limited. In a federation like the USA or India, powers are split between the Union and States, and a constitution limits both. Austin's illimitable sovereign cannot be found.
    • International law. Calling it "positive morality" ignores how States actually treat it as binding.
    • Continuity. If law is the command of this sovereign, why does law survive the death of the king who made it? Hart's Rex I and Rex II example.

    Against "sanction"

    • People obey for many reasons: habit, respect, morality, convenience. Fear of punishment is only one. Duguit and the sociological school stressed this.
    • Some laws have no sanction at all, for example many constitutional provisions; and nullity (an invalid contract) is not really a "punishment".

    Against the method

    • Too narrow: it ignores the moral, social and historical life of law. Paranjape notes Austin's exclusion of morality made the school sterile.
    • Based on English law and Roman law alone, as they stood in the early nineteenth century.

    Austin and the Indian Constitution

    Try to find an Austinian sovereign in India and you will fail. Parliament's power is limited by Part III (Article 13 makes laws inconsistent with fundamental rights void), by the distribution of powers in Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule, and by judicial review. Even Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 is limited by the basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, 1973). The Preamble places ultimate authority in "We, the People of India", which is exactly the kind of indeterminate body Austin said could not be sovereign.

    Exam lens

    Do not end an Austin answer on criticism alone. Give him his due: he brought clarity, precision and a method; he separated law from morals in a way that made law easier to identify; and most of his critics, including Hart, began by building on him. One line: "Austin's theory is incomplete, but nearly every later theory of law starts by correcting it."

    Hart: the concept of law

    H.L.A. Hart (1907–1992), Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford, revived positivism after the Second World War. In The Concept of Law (1961) he rejected Austin's model while keeping the separation thesis. His starting point is a famous image: Austin's theory reduces law to the gunman situation writ large. A robber who says "your money or your life" gives an order backed by a threat; you are obliged to hand over your money. But you do not have an obligation to do so. Law creates obligations; the gunman only obliges.

    Why commands fail, according to Hart

    • Variety of laws. Many laws confer powers rather than impose duties: to make contracts, wills and marriages; to legislate; to adjudicate. Treating them as fragments of duty-imposing commands distorts them.
    • Range of application. Laws bind those who make them.
    • Mode of origin. Custom is law without being anyone's command.
    • Continuity and persistence. When Rex I dies, Rex II can legislate at once, and Rex I's laws survive. Habits of obedience cannot explain this; only a rule conferring authority can.

    Primary and secondary rules

    Hart's alternative is that law is the union of primary and secondary rules. Primary rules impose duties: do not steal, pay your taxes. A society could survive on primary rules alone (a "pre-legal" society), but it would suffer three defects. Each secondary rule is the remedy for one.

    Defect of primary rules aloneWhat goes wrongThe remedy: a secondary rule
    UncertaintyNo one can say for sure which rules are the rulesRule of recognition: identifies which rules are valid law
    Static qualityRules change only by slow growth and decayRule of change: empowers people to make, amend and repeal rules (legislation, contracts, wills)
    InefficiencyNo authority decides when a rule has been brokenRule of adjudication: empowers judges to decide disputes and impose sanctions

    The rule of recognition and the internal point of view

    The rule of recognition is the master rule of a legal system. It sets the criteria of validity for all other rules: in the United Kingdom, roughly "whatever the Queen in Parliament enacts is law". Its own existence is a matter of social fact: it exists because officials, especially judges, actually accept and use it. It is not valid or invalid; it simply is, or is not, practised.

    That acceptance is what Hart calls the internal point of view. An observer can record that drivers stop at red lights (external point of view). A participant treats the red light as a reason to stop and criticises those who don't (internal point of view). For a legal system to exist, Hart says, two minimum conditions must be met: private citizens must generally obey the valid primary rules, and officials must accept the secondary rules from the internal point of view.

    Open texture and the minimum content of natural law

    Rules are expressed in general words, and words have a "core of settled meaning" and a "penumbra of uncertainty". A rule says "no vehicles in the park". A car is clearly a vehicle; but a bicycle, a toy car, a war memorial tank? In the penumbra, Hart says, judges must exercise discretion, and in doing so they make law.

    Hart also conceded, as you saw in Unit I, a minimum content of natural law. Given five truisms about human beings (vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, limited understanding and strength of will), every workable legal system must protect persons, property and promises. This is a concession about content, not validity: Hart still held that an iniquitous law can be valid law.

    The Hart–Fuller debate

    In 1958 the Harvard Law Review (vol. 71) published two essays that became the classic modern exchange between positivism and natural law: Hart's "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" and Lon Fuller's "Positivism and Fidelity to Law: A Reply to Professor Hart".

    The test case

    The grudge informer

    Germany, 1944 and after the war

    A woman wanting to be rid of her husband reported his remarks critical of Hitler. Under Nazi statutes he was sentenced to death (later sent to the front instead). After the war she was prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of liberty. Her defence: she had acted under laws valid at the time. A post-war court convicted her, reportedly on the ground that the Nazi statutes were contrary to the sound conscience of all decent human beings.

    Hart

    It was law, but too evil to obey

    Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals

    Hart said the honest course is to admit that the Nazi statute was law, and then, if punishment was right, to pass an openly retroactive statute. Saying "it was never law" hides a hard moral choice behind a confusion of concepts. Separating law and morals keeps moral criticism of law clear and sharp.

    Fuller

    It was not law at all

    Positivism and Fidelity to Law

    Fuller replied that law has an inner morality. The Nazi regime used secret laws, retroactive laws and ignored its own rules so grossly that it ceased to be a legal system. Fidelity to law is owed only to something that deserves the name. Fuller later developed this into the eight principles of The Morality of Law (1964).

    Hart faced a second challenge from his successor at Oxford, Ronald Dworkin, who argued in Taking Rights Seriously (1977) that law contains not only rules but principles (such as "no one may profit from his own wrong", applied in Riggs v. Palmer, 1889, to stop a murderer inheriting from his victim). Judges in hard cases do not have strong discretion; they are bound by principles. In the posthumous Postscript (1994) Hart accepted that a rule of recognition may include moral criteria, a position called "soft" or inclusive positivism.

    Kelsen's pure theory of law

    Hans Kelsen (1881–1973), an Austrian jurist, drafted the Austrian Constitution of 1920 and later taught at Berkeley. His Pure Theory of Law (Reine Rechtslehre, 1934; second edition 1960) and General Theory of Law and State (1945) set out the most rigorous form of positivism ever attempted.

    Why "pure"?

    Kelsen wanted a theory of law free from ethics, politics, sociology, psychology and history. Those disciplines study real things; law must be studied on its own terms. Justice, for Kelsen, is an irrational ideal; it belongs to politics, not to legal science.

    Law as a system of norms

    Law consists of norms, and a norm is an "ought" (sollen) proposition, not an "is" (sein). Its form is: if a person commits theft, then an official ought to impose a sanction. So the primary norm, for Kelsen, is addressed to officials: it stipulates the sanction. Like Austin, he gives coercion a central place; unlike Austin, he removes the psychology of "wish" and "fear" from the picture.

    A norm is valid only because a higher norm authorises it. A contract is valid because the Contract Act authorises contracts; the Act is valid because the Constitution authorises Parliament; the Constitution is valid because… this is where the chain must stop.

    The Grundnorm

    At the top sits the Grundnorm, the basic norm. It is not made by anyone: it is presupposed. In effect it says, "the first historical constitution ought to be obeyed". It cannot be validated by a higher norm, because there is none. Two features matter for the exam:

    • It is a hypothesis, not a fact. Jurists assume it so that the whole system can be read as valid.
    • It must be efficacious, "by and large". Kelsen says a legal order is valid only if, on the whole, it is actually effective. A Grundnorm that nobody follows will not be presupposed. This is where Kelsen lets the real world back in, and it is the key to the revolution cases below.

    The hierarchy of norms

    Below the Grundnorm, norms form a hierarchy (Stufenbau, a stepped structure, an idea developed with his colleague Adolf Merkl). Law moves from the general to the particular by concretisation: each level both applies the norm above and creates a more specific norm below. A judgment or an administrative order is an "individual norm", the last step. Trace one rule to its root:

    Kelsen and the Indian Constitution

    Indian writers have debated what India's Grundnorm is. The most common answer is that the Constitution itself is at the top of the hierarchy of posited norms, and the Grundnorm is the presupposition that it ought to be obeyed. Others point to the Preamble and "We, the People" as the political source.

    The more useful exam point is how neatly the basic structure doctrine fits Kelsen's hierarchy. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a thirteen-judge bench held by 7:6 that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 does not extend to altering the basic structure of the Constitution. A constitutional amendment, in other words, is itself a norm whose validity is tested against a higher norm. Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) struck down clause (4) of Article 329A on that ground, and Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) struck down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368, inserted by the 42nd Amendment, which tried to make the amending power unlimited. Each is a court refusing to let a lower norm escape the norm above it.

    In plain hierarchy terms, Indian law looks like this: the Constitution (with its basic structure) → Acts of Parliament and State Legislatures, and Ordinances → rules, regulations and bye-laws made under them → executive orders, decrees and judgments, the individual norms. A rule at any level that goes beyond the authority of the level above is ultra vires and void.

    Criticism of Kelsen

    • The Grundnorm is vague and fictional. Kelsen never says clearly what it is in a given system; critics call it a jurist's assumption doing too much work.
    • Efficacy smuggles in fact. If the Grundnorm depends on being effective "by and large", the pure theory rests on a sociological question after all. Its purity is incomplete.
    • Sanction-centred. Like Austin, Kelsen struggles with power-conferring rules, and treats every norm as an instruction to officials.
    • Ignores values. By banishing justice, the theory has nothing to say when law becomes monstrous. Laski called it "a magnificent exercise in logic"; Paranjape adds that it is sterile in practice.
    • International law. Kelsen preferred monism with the primacy of international law, which many States have never accepted.

    Merits: it gives the clearest picture of a legal system as a hierarchy, it explains ultra vires and judicial review, and it has deeply influenced constitutional courts, which Kelsen himself helped invent in Austria.

    Revolutions and the Grundnorm

    What happens when a coup overthrows a constitution? Kelsen's answer follows from efficacy. If the revolution succeeds, a new Grundnorm is presupposed and the new regime's orders are valid law; if it fails, the revolutionaries are traitors. Several courts in the Commonwealth were faced with exactly this question, and their answers are a favourite short-note topic.

    The lesson the courts drew in the end is the one most examiners expect. Kelsen offered a theory for describing a legal order after the event; he did not offer judges a licence to legitimise a usurper while the struggle is still going on. The Asma Jilani court said exactly that, and later courts in Pakistan swung between that view and the "doctrine of necessity" (Begum Nusrat Bhutto v. Chief of Army Staff, 1977).

    An honest assessment

    Positivism's great strength is clarity. It tells a lawyer where to look to find the law, and it keeps the moral question open instead of answering it in advance. Hart's argument is strong: if you say an evil law "is not law", you risk making people think every law that is law must be good.

    Its weaknesses are just as clear. Austin's sovereign does not fit a modern constitution, least of all India's. Kelsen's purity gives way at the very point where it is most needed, the moment of revolution. And by treating law as a closed system of rules, positivism has little to say about how law grows, how judges actually decide, or how law works in society. The next four chapters are each, in one way, a reply to that silence: the historical school asks where law comes from, the realists ask what courts actually do, the sociological school asks what law is for, and the critical scholars ask whose interests it serves.
